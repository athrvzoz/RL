01The big picture

You already know one TD method. You just didn't know its family name.

In project 3 you wrote this line: target = r + γ·max Q[s2]. You updated after every step, using your own guess for the next state. That trick has a name: Temporal-Difference (TD) learning. "Temporal difference" means: the difference between what you guessed now and what you guess one step later.

Q-learning is one member of the TD family. This booklet introduces its siblings:

  • TD(0): the simplest one. Learns V(s), "how good is this square?", for a fixed way of playing.
  • SARSA: learns Q(s,a) like Q-learning, but uses the action it will really take next instead of the best action.
  • Expected SARSA: uses the average over the actions it might take next.
The one sentence

Q-learning learns how good things are if you play perfectly. SARSA learns how good things are for the way you actually play, including your random exploration mistakes. On a cliff edge, that difference decides whether you walk along the edge or keep your distance.

03Intuition: guessing your arrival time

TD is how you already think when you travel.

You leave school at 4:00 and guess: "I'll be home at 4:40."

MomentWhat happenedNew guessMonte Carlo would…TD does…
4:00 leave school—4:40wait—
4:10 reach bus stopbus is late4:50wait"my 4:00 guess was 10 min too hopeful" → fix it now
4:25 off the bustraffic was light4:45wait"my bus-stop guess was 5 min too pessimistic" → fix it now
4:45 homearrived—finally fix all guesseslast small fix

TD learns at every stop from the change between two guesses. You don't need to reach home to learn that the bus being late was bad news. That's the whole idea.

Why the bold learner and the careful learner differ

Imagine walking along a cliff path to school. You are a little clumsy: 1 step in 10 goes in a random direction (that's ε = 0.1).

The bold planner (Q-learning)

"The edge path is shortest. If I walk perfectly, it's the best." It plans for its perfect self. But it is clumsy, so in practice it falls off sometimes.

The careful planner (SARSA)

"I know I stumble. Near the edge one stumble = disaster. I'll walk two rows away." It plans for its real, clumsy self. Longer walk, almost no falls.

Neither is "wrong". They answer different questions. You'll see both, with real numbers, in chapter 09.

04TD(0): learning V step by step

td_prediction.py · the 5-square random walk

          0      +----+----+----+----+----+      +1
       [LEFT] <- | A  | B  | C  | D  | E  | -> [RIGHT]
                 +----+----+----+----+----+
                             start            each step: coin flip, left or right

Nobody chooses anything here: a coin decides each move. This is prediction: just learn V(s) = "chance of finishing on the right". The true answers are known: A = 1/6, B = 2/6, C = 3/6, D = 4/6, E = 5/6. That lets us measure how wrong each method still is.

The TD(0) update

V(s)  ←  V(s)  +  α · [ r  +  γ · V(s′)  −  V(s) ]
                        └──── target ────┘
                        └──────────── δ (TD error) ───────┘

It's the same shape as project 3's Q-learning line, with V instead of Q and no max (nobody chooses actions here).

Monte Carlo moved every square at once, but it had to wait for the end, and it trusts one noisy game completely.

Why TD wins here

Monte Carlo's target is the final result of one whole random game: very noisy (a C-start game can end 0 or 1 by pure luck). TD's target is one step plus a guess: less noisy, but it starts out biased (the guesses are wrong at first). On this problem the low noise wins. People call this bias vs variance.

05From V to Q: SARSA

Now the agent chooses actions, so we learn Q(s, a), exactly like project 3.

The SARSA update

Q(s, a)  ←  Q(s, a)  +  α · [ r  +  γ · Q(s′, a′)  −  Q(s, a) ]
                                         ▲
                     a′ = the action you WILL actually take in s′
                          (already chosen by ε-greedy, maybe a random one)

Compare with Q-learning, which you already know:

Q(s, a)  ←  Q(s, a)  +  α · [ r  +  γ · maxa Q(s′, a)  −  Q(s, a) ]
                                         ▲
                     the BEST action in s′, whether you'll take it or not

Why the order of steps changes

SARSA needs a′ before it can update. So it chooses the next action first, uses it in the target, and then really takes it on the next step:

On-policy vs off-policy, the cleanest explanation

Q-learning (off-policy)SARSA (on-policy)
How it actually playsε-greedy (sometimes random)ε-greedy (sometimes random)
Whose values it learnsa perfect greedy player, who never exploresitself, random moves included
Question it answers"How good is this if I play perfectly afterwards?""How good is this given how I really play?"
Can learn from someone else's games?yes (the max ignores which action was taken next)no, needs a′ from its own policy
Two kinds of probability, don't mix them

The world's probabilities (FrozenLake's slip chances) are rules of the environment; TD methods never see them. π(a|s) is the agent's own dice: "with ε = 0.1 and 4 actions I pick the best move 92.5% of the time and each other move 2.5%". Expected SARSA uses only this second kind, and it knows it exactly because it's its own rule. (Where 92.5% comes from: each of the 4 actions gets ε/4 = 0.025; the best one also gets 1 − ε = 0.9, so 0.925.)

The Expected SARSA future, 0.05·2 + 0.85·5 + 0.05·1 + 0.05·0, is a dot product π · Q(s′), the school maths you know. In the code it's literally np.dot(policy_probs(Q, s2, eps), Q[s2]).

Q-learning always assumes the best. SARSA usually agrees, but sometimes (when it explores) drops sharply, and on average it equals Expected SARSA, which gives that average every time, with no jumps. That's why Expected SARSA's curve is the smoothest in chapter 09.

07The algorithm

SARSA's loop. Spot the one difference from project 3: the next action is chosen before the update.

Pseudocode: three methods, one loop

Q = 0 everywhere
for each game:
    s = reset();  a = ε-greedy(Q, s)
    until the game ends:
        s2, r = step(a)
        a2 = ε-greedy(Q, s2)
        if   SARSA:           future = Q[s2, a2]
        elif Expected SARSA:  future = Σ π(b|s2) · Q[s2, b]     (dot product)
        elif Q-learning:      future = max Q[s2]
        target = r + γ · future          (just r if the game ended)
        Q[s, a] += α · (target − Q[s, a])
        s, a = s2, a2

08The code, line by line

td_agents.py: all three methods in one file.

① The agent's own dice: policy_probs

def policy_probs(Q, s, eps):
    n = Q.shape[1]
    probs = np.full(n, eps / n)                       # every action: the explore share
    best = np.flatnonzero(Q[s] == Q[s].max())
    probs[best] += (1 - eps) / len(best)              # best action(s): + the exploit share
    return probs

② The ONE line where the methods differ: future_value

def future_value(method, Q, s2, a2, eps):
    if method == "sarsa":
        return Q[s2, a2]                              # what I will actually do next
    if method == "expected_sarsa":
        return np.dot(policy_probs(Q, s2, eps), Q[s2])  # average over my own dice
    if method == "qlearning":
        return np.max(Q[s2])                          # the best I could do next

③ The training loop

for ep in range(episodes):
    s, _ = env.reset()
    a = epsilon_greedy(Q, s, eps, rng)            # SARSA needs the first action before the loop
    done = False
    while not done:
        s2, r, terminated, truncated, _ = env.step(a)
        a2 = epsilon_greedy(Q, s2, eps, rng)      # choose the NEXT action now
        target = r + gamma * future_value(method, Q, s2, a2, eps) * (not terminated)
        td_error = target - Q[s, a]               # the surprise, δ
        Q[s, a] += alpha * td_error               # update the state we LEFT
        s, a = s2, a2                             # the next action is already chosen
        done = terminated or truncated
LineMeaning
a = epsilon_greedy(...) before the loopSARSA always holds "the action I'm about to take". It needs one before the first step.
a2 = epsilon_greedy(Q, s2, …)Choose the next move now, possibly a random explore move. All three methods do this, but only SARSA puts it in the target.
future_value(...)The only difference between the three methods.
* (not terminated)Reached the goal → no future → target = r. (Falling in the cliff does not end CliffWalking; it sends you back to S with −100.)
Q[s, a] += alpha * td_errorExactly project 3's nudge: the state you left is updated; s2 is only read.
s, a = s2, a2The chosen a2 is the action really played next. That's what makes SARSA "on-policy".

An honest detail

In 7 of 50 runs SARSA's greedy arrows walked in a circle and never reached G within 100 steps. With α = 0.5 and random a′ in the target, SARSA's values jump around (example 1: 3.25 or 1.00 for the same move), and some runs end on a messy table. Expected SARSA removes that randomness, and all 50 runs found the same safe 15-step path. Chapter 10 shows that lowering α also fixes SARSA.

11Real-world problems

When does "learn about how I really behave" matter? When mistakes during learning cost something real.

Rule of thumb

Use SARSA / Expected SARSA when the agent learns while acting for real and keeps exploring or keeps making small errors. Use Q-learning when learning happens in a safe simulator, or from logged data, and the final agent won't explore.

1 · A warehouse robot near a loading dock

Staterobot's grid cell, whether it carries a box, battery level (discretised)
Actionup / down / left / right / pick / drop
Reward−1 per move, +20 per delivered box, −200 for driving off the dock edge (damage)
Episodeone delivery shift
Why SARSAIts wheels slip and its sensors are noisy, so it never moves perfectly. Like the cliff, SARSA learns to keep a margin from the dock edge. Q-learning would plan the edge-hugging route for a perfect robot that doesn't exist.
PitfallReal crashes during learning are expensive: start in simulation, then fine-tune on the real robot with small ε.

2 · A thermostat learning in a real office building

Stateroom temperature band, outside temperature band, hour of day, occupancy
Actionheater/AC setting: off / low / medium / high
Reward−(energy cost) − 10 × (degrees outside the comfort band)
Episodeone day
Why Expected SARSAIt must keep exploring a little to adapt to the seasons, and every exploratory action is felt by real people. Expected SARSA learns safe settings that remain comfortable even when it explores, and it's stable with a larger α.
PitfallDiscretising temperature too coarsely hides important states. Too finely and the Q-table becomes huge (booklet 03, DQN, solves that).

3 · A beginner trading bot with real money

Stateprice trend (down / flat / up), current position (none / holding), time to market close
Actionbuy / hold / sell
Rewardprofit or loss of each step; large penalty if the loss for the day exceeds a limit
Why on-policyThe bot keeps making exploratory trades, and those trades lose real money. SARSA prices in its own exploration and avoids strategies that are only good for a perfect trader.
PitfallMarkets change (the "rules" drift) and are very noisy. In practice you would first learn offline from historical data, which is an off-policy setting, so a Q-learning-style method fits there. Treat any trading example as a learning exercise, not advice.

4 · Learning from a driving simulator vs from a log of human drivers

If you only have recorded data of other drivers (you can't choose actions), you need an off-policy method: Q-learning's max doesn't care which action was taken next, so it can learn from anyone's experience. SARSA needs the next action from its own policy, so it can't use other people's logs directly. That's the practical side of on-policy vs off-policy.

13Clearing up the confusions

Short, correct answers to the questions beginners ask most.

Is Q-learning a TD method?

Yes. Anything that updates after each step using its own guess of the next state is TD. Q-learning, SARSA and TD(0) are all TD methods.

What does "temporal difference" actually mean?

The difference between two guesses made at different times: Q(s,a) now, and r + γ·(guess at s′) one step later. That difference is δ.

Which state does SARSA update?

The state you left, for the action you took: Q[s, a]. s2 and a2 are only read, exactly as in project 3.

Does SARSA take a2 twice: once in the target and once for real?

No. It chooses a2 once, uses its Q-value in the target, and then really plays that same a2 on the next step (s, a = s2, a2).

Does SARSA know the cliff probabilities?

No. CliffWalking's rules are hidden, as in FrozenLake. The only probabilities SARSA "knows" are its own ε-greedy choices, and plain SARSA doesn't even compute those; it just uses the a2 it rolled.

Then why does SARSA avoid the cliff if nobody told it about stumbling?

Because sometimes its a2 really is a random "down" on the edge, and that −114 value goes into the target. Over thousands of updates, the edge squares' values absorb that risk.

Q-learning also explores. Why doesn't it learn the danger?

It does fall in during training (179 times!), and the "step down" Q-value becomes very negative. But its target uses max, which never picks that bad action, so the edge squares themselves still look fine.

So is Q-learning wrong on the cliff?

No. It correctly learned the best path for a player who doesn't explore (greedy test −13, the best possible). It's "wrong" only for the agent that keeps exploring.

If ε goes to 0, are SARSA and Q-learning the same?

With ε = 0, a2 is always the greedy action, so Q[s2, a2] = max Q[s2] and the updates become identical. Experiment 2 shows them getting close as ε shrinks.

Why is Expected SARSA better than SARSA here?

Same average target, but no randomness from which a2 was rolled. Less noise → smoother learning, works even with α = 1.0. It costs one small dot product per step.

Why was SARSA's greedy path a loop in 7 runs?

Its values were still jumpy (α = 0.5 + random a2). With the arrows read greedily, some squares pointed at each other. Lower α (0.1) or Expected SARSA fixes it.

Is TD always better than Monte Carlo?

No. TD has lower noise but starts biased by wrong guesses. On the random walk TD won; in some problems (e.g. when the state description is incomplete) MC can be better. n-step methods sit in between, and a middle n often wins.

What do γ = 1 and "the cliff doesn't end the game" change?

γ = 1: future steps count fully (fine, because every game eventually reaches G). Falling sends you back to S with −100 but the game continues, so terminated is only true at G.

The only line that changes 
SARSA:          Q[s2, a2]
Expected SARSA: Σ π(b|s2)·Q[s2,b]
Q-learning:     max Q[s2]
TD(0) (for V):  V(s2)
Monte Carlo:    (no guess) real G
Four sentences to remember
  1. TD methods update after every step using their own guess of the next state; Q-learning is one of them.
  2. SARSA's target uses the action you will really take; Q-learning's uses the best action. That is on-policy vs off-policy.
  3. On the cliff, SARSA learns a safe path for a clumsy explorer; Q-learning learns the shortest path for a perfect player.
  4. Expected SARSA averages over your own action probabilities (a dot product), giving SARSA's caution with much less noise.
