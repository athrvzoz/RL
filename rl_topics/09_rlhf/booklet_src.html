01The big picture

A language model is a policy. Writing a reply is an episode.

A language model writes one word (token) at a time, choosing each from a softmax over its vocabulary: exactly the policies of booklets 04–06. In RL words:

RL wordFor a chat assistant
statethe prompt + everything written so far
actionthe next word (or "stop")
policy π(a|s)the model's probability for each next word
episodeone complete reply
rewardone number for the whole reply, given only at the end: "how good was this answer?"

The hard part is the reward: nobody can write a formula for "a good answer". RLHF's trick is to learn the reward from people's comparisons.

The one sentence

RLHF trains a reward model to predict which of two replies people prefer (Bradley–Terry), then fine-tunes the language model with PPO to earn high reward-model scores, while a KL penalty keeps it close to the original model so it can't "hack" the reward model's blind spots.

03Intuition: comparing is easier than scoring

Judging an essay competition

Ask a teacher "give this essay a score out of 100" and different teachers say 62, 75, 81: very inconsistent. Ask "which of these two essays is better?" and they mostly agree. People are much better at comparing than at scoring. RLHF collects thousands of comparisons and learns a scoring rule that explains them.

The danger: teaching to the rubric

Suppose students learn the teacher's marking rule is "longer essays tend to win". A clever student writes 20 pages of filler and scores well, but the essay is bad. The marking rule (the reward model) was a shortcut that worked for normal essays, and it breaks when someone optimises against it. That's reward hacking, and our toy shows it happening (chapter 10).

Goodhart's law

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." The reward model measures quality on the kind of replies it saw. Push the policy hard to maximise it, and the policy moves to replies the measure was never tested on.

The fix: a leash

The KL penalty says: "you may change your style, but every step away from your original (SFT) way of writing costs you". Small, sensible improvements pay off; drifting into strange text costs more than the reward model gives.

One policy-gradient step on a 3-word softmax row

logits [0, 0, 0] → probabilities [0.333, 0.333, 0.333]

gradient of log π = one_hot − π = [−0.333, +0.667, −0.333]
new logits = old + 0.3 × 1.0 × gradient = [−0.1, 0.2, −0.1]

PPO's clip (ε = 0.2): stop pushing after a 20% change

ratio 1.1, A +2.0:  min(+2.20, +2.20) = +2.20   (normal push)
ratio 1.3, A +2.0:  min(+2.60, +2.40) = +2.40   (clipped: stop pushing)
ratio 0.7, A −2.0:  min(−1.40, −1.60) = −1.60   (clipped: stop pushing)
ratio 0.7, A +2.0:  min(+1.40, +1.60) = +1.40   (normal push: fix a wrong-way change)

In RLHF, every word of a reply gets the same advantage: the reward arrives only at the end (like REINFORCE's Monte Carlo return), minus a baseline, the average reward of the other replies to the same prompt.

06Maths I: Bradley–Terry, the reward model

The maths: the exponential, the logarithm, and the chain rule.

P(A preferred over B)  =  σ( rA − rB )          σ(x) = 1 / (1 + e−x)
                              ▲ the reward model's scores for the two replies

loss for one pair where W won and L lost  =  − log σ( rW − rL )
  • Equal scores → σ(0) = 0.5: a coin flip. A score gap of 2 → σ(2) = 0.88: 88% sure.
  • Only differences matter: adding 100 to every score changes nothing.
  • Minimising the loss = making the observed winners look likely: "maximum likelihood".

Worked example (real output)

reward model says r_A = 2.0, r_B = 1.0          d = r_A − r_B = 1.0
P(A preferred) = σ(1.0) = 1 / (1 + e−1) = 1 / (1 + 0.3679) = 0.7311

If the human picked A:  loss = −log(0.7311) = 0.3133
   slope d(loss)/d(r_A) = −(1 − σ(d)) = −0.2689   → raise r_A
   slope d(loss)/d(r_B) = +(1 − σ(d)) = +0.2689   → lower r_B
If the human picked B (the model was WRONG):  loss = −log σ(−1) = 1.3133,  slope −0.7311: a big push

One gradient step (learning rate 0.5) after "A won":  r_A 2.0 → 2.1345,   r_B 1.0 → 0.8655
   new P(A preferred) = 0.7806,  new loss = 0.2477

Where the slope comes from (chain rule)

d/dx [ −log σ(x) ]  =  −σ′(x)/σ(x)  =  −σ(x)(1 − σ(x))/σ(x)  =  −(1 − σ(x))      using σ′ = σ(1 − σ)
x = rW − rL:   ∂loss/∂rW = −(1 − σ(x)),    ∂loss/∂rL = +(1 − σ(x))
gap d−2−10124
σ(d)0.1190.2690.5000.7310.8810.982
loss2.1271.3130.6930.3130.1270.018
slope−0.881−0.731−0.500−0.269−0.119−0.018

Pairs the model already gets right with a big gap barely move it; pairs it gets wrong push hardest. Same idea as the TD error: learn most from surprises.

The blind spot (the reward model has never seen repetition) 
sorry refund                                        RM +0.65   true +4.0

Adding up word weights, the more "refund"s the better, while real humans hate the 8-fold repetition. Real reward models are big neural networks with subtler blind spots (for example favouring longer or more flattering answers), but the problem is the same.

08Maths II: KL, staying close to home

KL(πnew ‖ πref) = Σa πnew(a) · log( πnew(a) / πref(a) )          0 = no drift at all

Worked example (real output): one word choice

small change:  π_ref = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2]  →  π_new = [0.4, 0.5, 0.1]      (sorry, refund, great)
    sorry : 0.40 × log(0.40/0.50) = 0.40 × (−0.2231) = −0.0893
    refund: 0.50 × log(0.50/0.30) = 0.50 × (+0.5108) = +0.2554
    great : 0.10 × log(0.10/0.20) = 0.10 × (−0.6931) = −0.0693
    KL = 0.0968
big change:    π_ref = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2]  →  π_new = [0.05, 0.9, 0.05]          KL = 0.8043

The reward used during RL

R(y) = rRM(y)  −  β · log( πnew(y) / πref(y) )
       ▲ the judge's score    ▲ "how much more likely is this reply now than for the SFT model?"
                                 (averaged over replies, this is exactly the KL)

reward model says 2.5;  π_new(y) = 0.4,  π_ref(y) = 0.02;   log ratio = log 20 = 2.9957
   β = 0.0 :  2.5 − 0.0 × 2.996 = +2.5000
   β = 0.1 :  2.5 − 0.1 × 2.996 = +2.2004
   β = 0.5 :  2.5 − 0.5 × 2.996 = +1.0021
   β = 1.0 :  2.5 − 1.0 × 2.996 = −0.4957      ← now this reply is NOT worth producing more of

A reply the SFT model would almost never write (2% likely) but the new model writes 40% of the time pays a big "drift tax". Unless the judge loves it a lot, it isn't worth it.

09RL fine-tuning: the algorithm and code

The code: rlhf_ppo.py

# 1. ROLL OUT: the policy writes a batch of replies (32 per prompt)
replies = sample_replies(theta, prompts, rng)
# 2. SCORE: reward model judges each finished reply, minus the KL penalty
logp_old = token_logprobs(theta, flat)
logp_ref = token_logprobs(theta_ref, flat)
log_ratio = np.bincount(flat[3], weights=logp_old - logp_ref, minlength=n)   # log π(y)/π_ref(y)
r_rm = np.array([features(p, to_words(r)) @ rm_w for p, r in zip(prompts, replies)])
R = r_rm - beta * log_ratio
# 3. ADVANTAGE: better or worse than the other replies to the same prompt?
for p in range(P):
    adv[prompts == p] -= R[prompts == p].mean()          # baseline = average reply
adv_tok = adv[flat[3]]                                   # every word shares its reply's advantage
# 4. PPO UPDATE: a few gradient steps on the clipped objective
for _ in range(epochs):
    ratio = np.exp(token_logprobs(theta, flat) - logp_old)
    active = np.where(adv_tok > 0, ratio < 1 + clip, ratio > 1 - clip)   # the clip, as on/off
    theta += lr * grad_of_logprob(theta, flat, adv_tok * ratio * active) / n
LineMeaning
theta = theta_ref.copy()RL starts from the SFT model; πref stays frozen as the anchor.
log_ratiolog π(y) = sum of the log-probabilities of its words, so log π(y)/πref(y) is a sum of per-word differences.
r_rm - beta * log_ratioChapter 08's penalised reward. β = 0: pure reward model.
active = ...PPO's clipped objective written as "push only while the ratio is inside the fence (in the helpful direction)".

What to notice

  • RL first helped, then hacked. With β = 0 the true score rose from +0.49 to +3.52 (iteration 70), better than any final result, before the policy discovered the blind spot and fell to −7. The judge was never fooled at first; it was fooled once the policy drifted far from the replies it was trained on.
  • β is the leash. β = 0.3 and 1.0 kept KL small (1.4 and 0.3) and ended up genuinely better than SFT (+1.9 and +1.8 vs +0.49). Too strong a leash (β = 1) limits improvement; too weak (β ≤ 0.1) lets the model hack.
  • The judge's score is not the goal. The run with the best reward-model score (+2.62) had the worst real quality. In practice, teams measure real quality separately (human evaluations), stop early, and refresh the reward model with new comparisons of the new model's replies.

11DPO: skip the reward model

Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023). Real output of python dpo.py.

DPO's insight: the best policy for "reward − β·KL" has a known shape, so you can rewrite the Bradley–Terry loss directly in terms of the policy's own probabilities. The policy is the reward model:

implicit reward of a reply:   r(y) = β · log( π(y) / πref(y) )

DPO loss for a pair (winner yw, loser yl):
   z    = β · [ log(π(yw)/πref(yw))  −  log(π(yl)/πref(yl)) ]
             ▲ how much MORE likely the winner became     ▲ … and the loser
   loss = − log σ(z)                                    ← the Bradley–Terry loss again!

Plain supervised learning on the fixed comparisons: no replies written during training, no reward model, no advantages, no clipping.

Worked example (real output, β = 0.5)

winner: log(0.10/0.08) = +0.2231        loser: log(0.05/0.06) = −0.1823
z = 0.5 × (0.2231 − (−0.1823)) = 0.2027
loss = −log σ(0.2027) = −log(0.5505) = 0.5969
push strength β·(1 − σ(z)) = 0.2247  → raise log π(winner), lower log π(loser)
(at the start π = π_ref, so z = 0 and loss = log 2 = 0.6931)

The DPO update in code

gain_w = reply_logprobs(theta, flat_w, n) - ref_w      # how much more likely each winner is now
gain_l = reply_logprobs(theta, flat_l, n) - ref_l      # ... and each loser
z = beta * (gain_w - gain_l)
push = beta * (1 - sigmoid(z))                         # big where the model is most wrong
grad = grad_of_logprob(theta, flat_w, push[...]) - grad_of_logprob(theta, flat_l, push[...])
theta += lr * grad / n                                 # gradient ASCENT on log σ(z)

  • In our toy, DPO beat PPO-based RLHF on true quality (+3.01 vs +1.94) with less drift (KL 0.92 vs 1.33), and it never hacked: it only learns from the human comparisons themselves, never from a reward model's blind spots.
  • DPO's hidden judge ranks held-out pairs 73–79% correctly, about as well as the separate reward model (71.6%).
  • Honest caveat: this is one toy. In large real systems both approaches are used and compared, and which is better depends on the data. PPO-style RLHF can use fresh replies and a reward model on new prompts; DPO is simpler and cheaper.

12Real-world problems

1 · Customer-support assistant (our toy, grown up)

State / actionconversation so far / next token

2 · A code assistant

Rewardmix of (a) automatic checks: does the code run and pass tests? and (b) a reward model from developers' preferences for readability
Why it helpstests are an objective part of the reward that's hard to hack; preferences cover style
Pitfallif the tests are weak, the model learns to pass tests rather than to write correct code (reward hacking again)

3 · A summariser (well documented)

OpenAI's "Learning to summarize from human feedback" (2020) trained a reward model from people's comparisons of summaries and fine-tuned with PPO + KL. The RL summaries were preferred over the supervised ones. When the KL penalty was too weak, they observed over-optimisation: summaries scored highly by the reward model but were worse according to people. That's the same story as our chapter 10.

4 · AI feedback (RLAIF) and Constitutional AI

Human comparisons are slow and expensive. In RLAIF another AI model makes the comparisons. Anthropic's Constitutional AI (2022) described models critiquing and comparing replies according to a written list of principles (a "constitution"), to train helpful and harmless assistants with far fewer human labels. The pipeline is the same; only the source of preferences changes.

Pitfalls in real life

Labeller disagreement: people genuinely differ (our labellers agree with the hidden taste only 88% of the time). Sycophancy: if people prefer answers that agree with them, the model learns to flatter. Reward hacking: any blind spot of the reward model gets found. Who chooses? The values in the preferences become the model's values, so choosing labellers and principles is an ethical decision, not just a technical one.

14Clearing up the confusions

Is the reward model the same as the language model?

No. It's a separate model that reads a reply and outputs one number. In real systems it's often a copy of the language model with its word-predicting output replaced by a single score.

Does a human give the reward during RL?

No. Humans only make comparisons beforehand. During RL the reward model gives every reward instantly, which is why it can be hacked: there's no human watching each reply.

Which probabilities are being learned?

The model's own next-word probabilities π(word | text so far): its action probabilities. Not the world's, and not the labellers' preference probabilities (those are only modelled by Bradley–Terry).

Why do all words in a reply get the same advantage?

The reward only arrives at the end of the reply, like a game with one prize at the finish (REINFORCE / Monte Carlo). Real systems sometimes add a critic to spread credit better.

Why not just maximise the reward model's score harder?

Because the reward model is only an approximation. Chapter 10: the hardest optimisation (β = 0) gave the best reward-model score and the worst real quality.

Is the KL penalty the same as PPO's clip?

Both keep changes small, but against different anchors. The clip limits each update relative to the policy that just wrote the batch. KL limits the total drift from the SFT model over the whole training.

Why is the reward model's accuracy only 72%?

Labellers are noisy: even judging with the hidden true score only reaches 88.1%, because people sometimes pick the worse reply. And our reward model is very simple (word counts).

Is DPO reinforcement learning?

It optimises the same goal as RLHF (reward − β·KL) but by supervised learning on fixed comparisons. It is often grouped with RLHF methods as "preference optimisation", without an RL loop.

Why does DPO's loss start at exactly 0.693?

At the start π = πref, so every gain is 0, z = 0, σ(0) = 0.5, and −log 0.5 = log 2 = 0.693: a coin flip.

Is this how every chat assistant is trained?

The general recipe (pretraining, then fine-tuning with demonstrations and preferences) is widely published. Exact details vary by company and change over time, and many use additional techniques. This booklet teaches the core, publicly documented ideas.

Four sentences to remember
  1. A language model is a policy: state = text so far, action = next word, reward = one score at the end of the reply.
  2. Nobody can write the reward, so a reward model learns it from human comparisons with the Bradley–Terry loss −log σ(rw − rl).
  3. PPO then maximises the reward model's score minus β·KL from the SFT model; without enough KL the policy hacks the reward model's blind spots.
  4. DPO skips the reward model and PPO: it applies the Bradley–Terry loss directly to the policy's own probability changes.
