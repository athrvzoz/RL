01The big picture

PPO = actor-critic + "never move the policy too far in one update".

In booklets 04 and 05 you saw policy methods collapse: REINFORCE dropped from 500 to below 200, and A2C fell from 371 to 48 around step 60,000. One unlucky, too-big update made the policy worse; the worse policy then collected worse games and learned worse lessons. A downward spiral.

PPO (Schulman et al., OpenAI, 2017) adds two ideas to actor-critic:

  • Reuse each batch of experience several times (10 passes here) instead of once. Far more learning per game played.
  • A fence: during those passes, no action's probability may change by more than about ±20% compared with the policy that collected the data. Beyond the fence, the update simply stops pushing.
The one sentence

PPO plays a batch of games, measures each move's advantage with a critic, and then improves the action probabilities over several passes, using the ratio r = πnew/πold and clipping it to [0.8, 1.2], so that no single update can change the policy too much.

PPO became the default because it's simple, works on discrete and continuous actions, and is hard to break. It was used for OpenAI Five (Dota 2) and for the RL step of InstructGPT and early ChatGPT, where the "policy" is a language model (booklet 09).

03Intuition: don't change your mind too much

The cricket batting coach, again

After one match the coach notices your pull shot scored well. A sensible coach says "play it a bit more often". A reckless coach says "play only pull shots from now on". The reckless advice is based on one match of evidence, in situations you happened to face. Next match the bowlers adapt and you collapse.

PPO is the sensible coach. The evidence (the rollout) was gathered with your old style. It's trustworthy for small changes to that style, not for a complete makeover. So:

  • Use the evidence several times (rewatch the match video 10 times), but
  • never let any shot become more than 20% more (or less) frequent from one match's evidence.

Why one batch is only trustworthy nearby

Small change: fine

The batch shows moves the old policy made and how they turned out. If the new policy still makes mostly those moves, the batch still describes it well.

Big change: blind

A very different policy visits different states. The batch has no evidence about them. Extrapolating far from your data is guessing.

04Recap: policy gradient and actor-critic

Booklets 04 and 05 in one page.

ACTOR   πθ(a|s): network → softmax (or a bell curve) → probabilities; play by rolling dice
CRITIC  V(s): network → one number, "total reward expected from here"

advantage   A = (what actually happened) − V(s)      "better or worse than expected?"
            1-step version:  A = δ = r + γ·V(s′) − V(s)

policy-gradient step:   θ ← θ + α · A · ∇log πθ(a|s)
    A > 0 → make a MORE likely;   A < 0 → make a LESS likely
critic step:            V(s) ← towards  r + γ·V(s′)        (squared-error loss)
MethodProblem PPO fixes
REINFORCENoisy (whole-game returns), one update per game, can take a ruinous step.
A2CUses each batch for one gradient step, then throws it away: wasteful. And still no protection against a big bad step.
PPO10 passes per batch (efficient) + the clip fence (safe).
Reminder: whose probabilities?

PPO learns the agent's action probabilities π(a|s). The ratio, the clip and the KL all talk about these, never about the world's randomness (slipping ice, wind).

05Maths I: the probability ratio

The maths: fractions, and log/exp.

r(θ) = πθ(a|s) / πold(a|s)          computed as  exp( log πθ(a|s) − log πold(a|s) )

r = 1.0   the action is exactly as likely as when it was played
r = 1.2   20% more likely now
r = 0.5   half as likely now

Real output of hand_examples.py: πold = 0.5, πnew = 0.6 → r = 0.6/0.5 = 1.20.

Why the ratio lets us reuse the batch

Policy gradient needs averages over actions drawn from the current policy. After the first update, the batch was drawn from πold, not πθ. Re-weighting each move by r corrects for that (it's the importance-sampling idea from Monte Carlo, booklet 02):

surrogate  L(θ) = average over the batch of  r(θ) · A

its gradient at θ = θold:   ∇r = ∇πθ/πold = ∇log πθ   (because πθ = πold there)
                           → exactly the ordinary policy gradient  A·∇log π

So at the start of the update, maximising r·A is the plain policy gradient. As θ moves away, the ratio keeps the estimate honest, but only while πθ stays close to πold. That's what the clip enforces.

06Maths II: the clipped objective

The heart of PPO. Do the table with a pencil.

LCLIP(θ) = average of   min(  r·A ,  clip(r, 1−ε, 1+ε)·A  )          ε = 0.2
                              ▲ normal push    ▲ same push, r held in [0.8, 1.2]

"Push good actions up and bad actions down, but once you've moved an action's probability by 20% in the right direction, stop. Wait for fresh evidence before going further."

07Maths III: GAE, the advantage dial

How good was each move? A blend of short and long looks, controlled by λ.

one-step surprise:    δt = rt + γ·V(st+1) − V(st)                      (TD error, booklets 01 and 05)
GAE, built backwards: At = δt + γλ·At+1
                      = δt + (γλ)·δt+1 + (γλ)²·δt+2 + …
critic target:        V-targett = At + V(st)

Worked example (real output): 3 steps, γ = 0.9, the game continues after

rewards r = [1, 0, 2]     critic V(s0..s3) = [3, 2, 4, 5]

δ0 = 1 + 0.9×2 − 3 = −0.20       δ1 = 0 + 0.9×4 − 2 = +1.60       δ2 = 2 + 0.9×5 − 4 = +2.50

λ = 0   : A = [−0.200, 1.600, 2.500]     (each move judged by its own one-step surprise)
λ = 0.95: A = [ 2.996, 3.738, 2.500]
λ = 1   : A = [ 3.265, 3.850, 2.500]     (= real rewards to the end of the rollout, then V)

Check λ = 0.95 by hand, backwards: A2 = 2.5; A1 = 1.6 + 0.9×0.95×2.5 = 1.6 + 2.1375 = 3.7375; A0 = −0.2 + 0.855×3.7375 = −0.2 + 3.196 = 2.996 ✓.

What the dial means

Move 0 had a slightly disappointing first step (δ0 = −0.2), but led into a run of good surprises. With λ = 0 it's judged bad (−0.2). With λ = 0.95 it gets credit for what followed (+3.0). λ trades bias (trusting a possibly wrong critic, λ small) against noise (adding up many random rewards, λ large). 0.95 is the usual choice: the same dial as n-step TD in booklet 01.

08Maths IV: the full loss, KL and clip fraction

total loss = − LCLIP          + 0.5 × (V(s) − V-target)²     − 0.01 × entropy
             ▲ actor (minus: ascend)    ▲ critic, squared error           ▲ stay a bit random

Two dials to watch while training

KL divergence, "how far did the policy move in this update?":

KL(πold ‖ πnew) = Σa πold(a) · ln( πold(a) / πnew(a) )        0 = no change

π_old = [0.5, 0.5] → π_new = [0.55, 0.45]   KL = 0.0050     (small step)
π_old = [0.5, 0.5] → π_new = [0.70, 0.30]   KL = 0.0872
π_old = [0.5, 0.5] → π_new = [0.95, 0.05]   KL = 0.8304     (a huge change of mind)

Check the first one: 0.5·ln(0.5/0.55) + 0.5·ln(0.5/0.45) = 0.5 × (−0.0953) + 0.5 × (0.1054) = 0.0050. The code uses a cheap approximation on the batch ("approx-KL"). Typical healthy PPO values are around 0.001–0.02.

Clip fraction: the share of moves whose ratio left [0.8, 1.2] during the update. Around 0.0–0.2 is normal. Near 0 means the fence is never touched (steps could be bigger); very high means the policy is straining against it every time.

09The algorithm

Pseudocode

actor πθ, critic V;   8 game copies
repeat:
    play 128 steps in each copy with πθ;  save s, a, log πold(a|s), r, ended, V(s)
    A, V-target = GAE(r, V, ended, γ = 0.99, λ = 0.95)
    for epoch = 1 … 10:
        shuffle the 1,024 moves into 8 minibatches
        for each minibatch:
            A ← (A − mean) / std
            r = exp(log πθ(a|s) − log πold(a|s))
            loss = −mean(min(r·A, clip(r, 0.8, 1.2)·A)) + 0.5·mean((V(s) − V-target)²) − 0.01·entropy
            gradient step (Adam, lr 3e-4, slowly lowered to 0)

10The code, line by line

ppo.py, shared by CartPole and Pendulum.

① GAE, walking backwards

for t in reversed(range(T)):
    alive = 1.0 - dones[t]                           # 0 if the game ended at step t
    delta = rewards[t] + gamma * next_value * alive - values[t]
    next_adv = delta + gamma * lam * alive * next_adv
    adv[t] = next_adv
    next_value = values[t]
returns = adv + values                               # target for the critic

② The clipped loss

d = agent.dist(obs)
new_logprobs = agent.log_prob(d, actions)
ratio = torch.exp(new_logprobs - old_logprobs)       # r = π_new(a|s) / π_old(a|s)
unclipped = ratio * adv
clipped = torch.clamp(ratio, 1 - clip, 1 + clip) * adv
policy_loss = -torch.min(unclipped, clipped).mean()  # minus: optimisers go DOWNHILL
value_loss = ((agent.value(obs) - returns) ** 2).mean()
total = policy_loss + vf_coef * value_loss - ent_coef * entropy

③ Several epochs over the same batch

for epoch in range(cfg["epochs"]):                       # 10 passes
    order = rng.permutation(n)                                # shuffle every epoch
    for start in range(0, n, mb_size):
        idx = order[start:start + mb_size]
        adv = (data["adv"][idx] - mean) / (std + 1e-8)        # normalise
        total, ... = ppo_loss(agent, ..., data["logprobs"][idx], adv, ...)
        optimizer.zero_grad(); total.backward()
        nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(agent.parameters(), 0.5)
        optimizer.step()
LineMeaning
old_logprobsSaved during the rollout with torch.no_grad(): πold stays frozen for all 80 gradient steps.
torch.exp(new − old)Division done with logs (safer with tiny probabilities): eln a − ln b = a/b.
torch.min(unclipped, clipped)Chapter 06's table: always take the more pessimistic value.
alive = 1 − dones[t]A finished game's future doesn't belong to the next game in the same copy, so cut the chain.
time-limit handling in the rolloutAs in A2C: at a time-out the future still counts, so γ·V(last state) is added to the reward.
anneal_lrThe learning rate slides from 3e-4 to 0 over training: big steps early, gentle at the end.
Normal(mean, log_std.exp())Continuous actions (Pendulum): a bell-curve policy instead of softmax; same loss, same clip.

What the four rows teach

  • No fence + big steps = disaster. Some action probabilities changed a million-fold in one update. The policy became certain of a terrible action, played only that, and the near-zero probabilities of everything else meant it could never discover anything better. Score 9 is worse than random (≈ 22).
  • The fence makes big steps safe. With the same ×10 learning rate, the clipped version learned fastest of all and was perfect on both seeds. The fence capped every move's change (largest ×4.2 over a whole update), so speed didn't turn into recklessness.
  • No fence + normal steps mostly works on easy CartPole, but its KL was 10–100× larger and one seed wobbled badly (dropping to about 50 mid-training). On harder tasks that's where runs die.
  • 2 seeds per variant: treat 446 vs 387 as a hint; 500 vs 9 as a fact.

Continuous actions: Pendulum (one torque in [−2, 2])

First 50 games: avg −1195    last 50 games: avg −189         (random play ≈ −1200, 0 = perfect)
Exam, 20 new games, centre of the bell curve: avg −172, best −0, worst −471
Learned bell-curve width (σ of the torque): 0.228 (started at 0.607)

chosen torque (bell centre), pendulum still:   −30° → +3.67   −10° → +1.78   0° → −0.04
                                               +10° → −1.77   +30° → −3.16   (clipped to ±2 by the game)
  • The learned rule is sensible: tilted left (−) → push +, tilted right → push −, upright → about 0.
  • The bell's centre can go beyond the legal range (+3.67); the game clips it to +2. PPO's Gaussian doesn't know the limits, which is one reason SAC (booklet 07) squashes with tanh.
  • The width σ shrank from 0.61 to 0.23: the policy became more certain as it learned (entropy fell from 0.94 to about −0.06; a bell curve's entropy can be negative).
  • Honest comparison: PPO needed about 300,000 steps; SAC/TD3 in booklet 07 reached similar or better scores in about 15,000 by reusing data from a replay buffer. On-policy methods pay for their stability with sample hunger.

13Real-world problems

When PPO fits

✔ You can generate lots of experience (a fast simulator, many copies). ✔ Discrete or continuous actions. ✔ You want a robust default that rarely blows up. ✘ Experience is expensive (a real robot, real money): an off-policy method (SAC) reuses data far better.

1 · Fine-tuning a chat assistant with human feedback (well documented)

Statethe prompt and the words written so far
Actionthe next token, from the language model's softmax: the language model is πθ
Rewardat the end: a reward model's score of the whole answer, minus a penalty for drifting too far from the original model (a KL penalty)
Why PPOA language model is a huge policy; one bad update could ruin its writing. The clip (plus the KL penalty) keeps every step small. InstructGPT (OpenAI, 2022) used exactly this; booklet 09 explains it in detail.
Pitfall"Reward hacking": the policy finds answers the reward model over-scores (e.g. very long ones). The KL penalty and careful reward models fight it.

2 · Game-playing teams (well documented)

Statethousands of numbers describing the game (units, health, positions)
Actionmove / attack / use ability, with targets: a large discrete action space
Rewardwin/loss plus shaped rewards (resources, kills)
Why PPOOpenAI Five trained with PPO on thousands of game copies in parallel through self-play, and beat the world champion team in Dota 2 (2019). PPO scales well to many parallel copies.

3 · A walking robot, trained in simulation first

Statejoint angles and speeds, body orientation, foot contacts
Actiontarget angle for each joint (continuous, Gaussian policy)
Reward+forward speed − energy − falling penalty
Why PPOPhysics simulators can run thousands of robots in parallel, so sample hunger doesn't matter, and PPO's stability does. Many legged-robot projects train with PPO in simulation, then transfer to the real robot ("sim-to-real") with randomised physics.
PitfallThe sim-to-real gap; and robots learn strange gaits if the reward doesn't penalise them.

15Clearing up the confusions

Is PPO value-based or policy-based?

Both: an actor-critic. The actor (policy) is what's being optimised; the critic only supplies advantages.

Does the clip stop the policy from ever changing more than 20%?

Per update, roughly, and only in the direction the advantage wants. Over many updates the policy can change completely. Also, the clip stops the push once past the fence; other moves sharing the weights can still nudge it a bit further (we saw ×2–4 at most).

Why take the min, not just the clipped value?

The min picks the pessimistic option. If an action moved the wrong way (a good action became less likely), the unclipped term is smaller and keeps the correction active. Clipping alone would also block those corrections.

Why is πold frozen?

It's the policy that actually played the batch. The ratio compares the improving policy to it; if it moved too, there would be no fixed point of reference.

Is the ratio the same as Monte Carlo's importance ratio ρ?

Same idea, one step at a time. Monte Carlo multiplied ratios over a whole game, which can explode; PPO uses one ratio per move and clips it, which keeps it tame.

What if the advantage is exactly 0?

r·0 = 0: no push either way. The move was exactly as good as the critic expected.

Why normalise advantages in each minibatch?

Their scale changes over training (early CartPole advantages ~+10, later smaller). Normalising keeps step sizes steady and splits each batch into "better than average" (+) and "worse than average" (−).

Why throw the batch away after 10 epochs?

After 10 epochs the policy has moved; the batch describes the old policy, and the ratio trick is only trustworthy nearby. Fresh data is needed. (That's the meaning of "on-policy".)

Entropy became negative on Pendulum. Is that a bug?

No. Entropy of a continuous bell curve is ½·ln(2πe·σ²), which is negative once σ < 0.24. Only discrete distributions have entropy ≥ 0.

Is PPO what ChatGPT-style assistants use?

PPO was the RL algorithm in the published InstructGPT recipe. Newer systems also use other methods (DPO, booklet 09, and variants). The core idea, careful policy improvement from a reward signal, remains.

PPO in 10 lines 
repeat:
  rollout 1,024 moves with π; save logπ_old
  A = GAE(δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s), λ)
  for 10 epochs, 8 shuffled minibatches:
    A = normalise(A)
    r = exp(logπ − logπ_old)

      + 0.5(V − target)² − 0.01·H

Key formulas 
r = π_new(a|s) / π_old(a|s)
L = min(r·A, clip(r,1−ε,1+ε)·A)
δ_t = r_t + γV(s_t+1) − V(s_t)
A_t = δ_t + γλ·A_t+1
V-target = A + V
KL = Σ π_old·ln(π_old/π_new)
Four sentences to remember
  1. PPO is an actor-critic that reuses each batch for several epochs, correcting with the ratio r = πnew/πold.
  2. The clipped objective min(r·A, clip(r)·A) stops pushing an action once its probability has moved ±20% in the direction the advantage wants.
  3. GAE's λ blends short (TD) and long (Monte Carlo) views of each move's advantage.
  4. Without the fence, big steps destroyed the policy (score 9, probabilities changed a million-fold); with it, the same big steps were safe (500).
