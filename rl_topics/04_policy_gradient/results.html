Rock-paper-scissors: learning probabilities

Against a biased opponent (rock 50%, paper 25%, scissors 25%), the agent's probabilities:

round    rock   paper  scissors
    0   0.333   0.333   0.333
  200   0.367   0.459   0.174
  500   0.147   0.798   0.055
 1000   0.050   0.919   0.031
 2000   0.015   0.975   0.010        average score over 2,000 rounds: +0.206 (always-paper: +0.250)

It discovered "paper beats their favourite rock". While learning it stays a little random, so it scores a bit less than the perfect +0.25.

Against the spy, which counters your most common move, the probabilities never settle: paper goes up → the spy plays scissors → scissors gets punished → rock goes up → … The largest probability after round 500 reached 0.984, and the score stayed near 0 (+0.040).

Per seed, with baseline: test scores 500, 500, 500, all three seeds perfect. Without a baseline: 103, 248, 409, a lottery.

What to notice

  • The baseline is the difference between working and not working. In CartPole every reward is +1, so without it every action is pushed up and the signal drowns in noise.
  • Returns-to-go matter even more. Crediting each action with the whole game's total (including rewards from before it) gave 32.5, barely above random (≈ 22).
  • Even the good version drops and recovers (games 220–320): one unlucky big update can make the policy worse. That's the problem PPO was invented to solve (booklet 06).

An honest lesson about generalisation

The policy is certain about the state it really meets (a pole spinning to the right → push right, 100%). But "leaning right while perfectly still" almost never happens in its own games: its pole is always moving a little. In those unfamiliar states its answers are unreliable, even though it scores a perfect 500 in real play. A network only learns well where it has experience, the same lesson as unvisited squares in FrozenLake. It also shows the policy mainly reacts to spin, not lean, which is a perfectly good strategy.
