01The big picture

If you remember only one page, remember this one.

In FrozenLake and Taxi the agent had a short list of actions: left, down, right, up. To act well it looked at one row of the Q-table and took the biggest number (argmax). To learn, it used max Q[s2]. Both need you to try every action and compare.

Now imagine the action is "how hard to push the motor": any number between −2 and +2. There is no row with 4 boxes. There are infinitely many torques: 0.5, 0.51, 0.5013… You can't try every number. This booklet is about the three methods that solve that problem.

What problem do they solve that your previous methods couldn't?

Where you used itQ-learning / DQN lineWhat breaks for a torque in [−2, 2]
Choosing an actionargmax(Q[s]): compare all actionsInfinitely many actions to compare
Bellman targetr + γ · max Q[s2]"max over all torques" is itself a search problem
Storing Qone box per (state, action)states and actions are both real numbers: no boxes
The one sentence

Instead of searching for the best action every time, train a second network, the actor, that outputs the best action directly, and improve it by asking the critic Q(s, a) "which way is uphill?" (that's the chain rule ∂Q/∂a · ∂a/∂θ).

And the three names in one line each

  • DDPG: deterministic actor μ(s) + critic Q(s, a). Explore by adding a little noise to the torque.
  • TD3: DDPG + twin critics (take the smaller), delayed actor updates, and smoothed targets. "Twin Delayed DDPG".
  • SAC: the actor outputs a bell curve of torques, not one torque, and gets a bonus reward for staying random ("staying curious"). Twin critics like TD3.
Our test bench: Pendulum-v1

State = [cos θ, sin θ, θ̇] (3 numbers). Action = 1 real number: torque.
Reward every step = −(θ² + 0.1·θ̇² + 0.001·a²): 0 when perfectly upright and still, about −16 at worst.
Game = 200 steps, then truncated (never "terminated").
The motor is too weak to lift the pole directly, so the agent must learn to swing it up, then balance.

02Recap: nets, replay, targets, actor & critic

The four ideas from DQN and actor-critic that this booklet builds on. Other booklets cover them in depth; here's what you need.

① A neural network is a Q-table that can fill in gaps

Your FrozenLake Q-table had 16 × 4 = 64 boxes. Pendulum's state is three real numbers, so there are infinitely many states. You can't have a box for each. A neural network is a function with adjustable numbers inside it (weights, called θ). You feed in numbers, it multiplies and adds, and out comes a number:

Learning = changing the weights so the output gets closer to a target. Instead of Q[s,a] += α·(target − Q[s,a]), we compute the loss (target − Q)², and gradient descent moves every weight a little in the direction that shrinks it. Same idea, "nudge toward the target", but it changes many weights at once, so it also changes the answers for similar states. That's the "fill in gaps" part.

② Replay buffer: a notebook of past moves

Every step we write one row (s, a, r, s2, done) into a big memory (100,000 rows). To learn, we pick 128 random rows and nudge on all of them. Why random? Consecutive steps are almost the same state (the pendulum moves a tiny bit each step). Learning from 128 nearly identical rows would push the net in one direction too hard. Random rows from many games are varied, like a teacher mixing questions from all chapters. Also, every row gets reused many times, so we need far fewer real steps.

③ Target network: don't chase your own tail

The Bellman target r + γ·Q(s2, …) uses the critic itself. If the same network both makes the target and moves toward it, every nudge also moves the target: like a dog chasing its tail. Fix: keep a slow copy of the network, the target network (written Q′ or Qtarget), and use only that copy to compute targets. DQN copies the weights over every few thousand steps. DDPG/TD3/SAC use a soft update instead: every step the copy moves just 0.5% of the way toward the real network (chapter 05 has the numbers).

④ Actor and critic: two jobs, two networks

Critic · "the judge"

Q(s, a): "if I'm in state s and push with torque a, then play well, how much total reward do I expect?" One number. Exactly the Q from Q-learning, but as a network that takes the torque as an input.

Actor · "the player"

μ(s): "in state s, push with this torque." It outputs an action. It replaces the argmax you used to choose moves. The critic tells it which way to change.

In A2C/PPO (other booklets) the actor outputs probabilities and learns from whole games it just played (on-policy). Here the actor learns from the critic's slope on rows from the replay buffer, including very old ones (off-policy). That's why these methods need far fewer steps.

Calculated vs given: the same rule as before

Given by the world: the reward r, the next state s2, whether the game ended. Calculated by us: the target, the critic's Q, the actor's torque. Learned (slowly changed): the weights θ of the actor and critic. Nobody gives the agent the physics of the pendulum: it is model-free, just like Q-learning.

04Intuition: you can't try every number

Why the argmax breaks, and the idea that replaces it.

Why not just chop the torque into pieces?

You could pretend the torque is a list: −2.0, −1.9, …, +2.0. That's 41 choices, and DQN could handle that for one motor. But real machines have many motors, and every motor's choices multiply (your code, hand_examples.py, part 1):

Motors (joints)Choices eachActions to compare in every argmax
1 (pendulum)4141
2411,681
34168,921
7 (a robot arm)41194,754,273,881

And steps of 0.1 are still rough: a steering angle of 0.73° vs 0.74° can matter. So we need a method where the action is calculated, not searched for.

Everyday analogy: the shower knob

You step into a hotel shower with a smooth knob. You don't try all 1,000 positions. You:

  1. turn it somewhere (actor: "my guess is here"),
  2. feel the water: "a bit too cold" (critic: a judgement, and crucially a direction),
  3. turn it a little toward hot (gradient ascent),
  4. repeat. Sometimes wiggle it to check (exploration noise).

The secret ingredient is in step 2: the feedback doesn't just say "good" or "bad", it says which way to turn. In maths, "which way is uphill, and how steep?" is a derivative. The critic is a smooth function of the torque, so we can take its derivative ∂Q/∂a. That's the whole trick.

Catch

The critic's "feel" is learned, so at first it is wrong. Actor and critic improve together, and most of TD3's and SAC's fixes are about not trusting a wrong critic too much.

The picture: list vs hill

Tiny hand example: one climb step

Suppose the critic (for one state) says Q(a) = 5 − (a − 2)², a hill with its top at a = 2. The actor currently says a = 0.5.

Q at a = 0.5        5 − (0.5 − 2)²  = 5 − 2.25 = 2.75
slope at a = 0.5    dQ/da = −2(a − 2) = −2(−1.5) = +3       positive: uphill is to the RIGHT
step (size 0.1)     a ← 0.5 + 0.1 × 3 = 0.8
Q at a = 0.8        5 − (0.8 − 2)²  = 5 − 1.44 = 3.56        higher than 2.75: it worked

Nobody tried a = 0.6, 0.7, … The slope alone said "go right, and quite a lot". That is the core of DDPG. In the real algorithm the actor is a network, so we nudge its weights, not the torque directly; chapter 05 adds that one extra link with the chain rule.

Connect it to what you know

In Q-learning, argmax(Q[s]) was a tiny "search". In DDPG the actor is that search, learned once and then answered instantly: μ(s) ≈ argmaxa Q(s, a). Everything else (Bellman target, replay, target network) stays as in DQN.

05DDPG maths, step by step

Three updates every step: the critic (Bellman, like Q-learning), the actor (chain rule), the targets (a weighted average). All numbers verified by hand_examples.py.

① The critic: Bellman, exactly as in Q-learning

y   =   r   +   γ · (1 − done) ·  Qtarget( s2 ,  μtarget(s2) )
▲       ▲       ▲   ▲               ▲                 ▲
TARGET  GIVEN   0.99 0 for          slow copy of      slow copy of the actor: its torque in s2
a number by the     Pendulum:       the critic,       REPLACES "max over actions"
        world       never ends      only READ

critic loss  =  average over the 128 rows of  ( Q(s, a) − y )²     ← gradient descent shrinks it

Compare with your Q-learning line target = r + γ · max Q[s2]. The only change: "max over all actions in s2" became "the torque the (target) actor would choose in s2". That's fine because the actor is trained to choose the torque with the highest Q, so μ(s2) ≈ argmax.

Worked example 1: one critic target (by hand)

Row from the buffer:  s, a, r = −0.5, s2           (Pendulum: never terminated, so done = 0)
Target actor in s2:   μtarget(s2) = some torque, and target critic says Qtarget(s2, that torque) = −20.0
TARGET    y = −0.5 + 0.99 × (−20.0) = −20.30
Critic now says Q(s, a) = −18.0
ERROR     y − Q = −20.30 − (−18.0) = −2.30         critic was too optimistic
LOSS      (−2.30)² = 5.29                         gradient descent lowers Q(s, a) toward −20.30
Which state is updated? (same answer as Q-learning)

The critic's weights change so that Q(s, a), the state you left with the torque you actually used (stored in the row), moves toward y. s2 is only read, through the slow target networks. And the row's torque a may be an old, noisy one: that's fine, Bellman holds for any action. That's what makes it off-policy.

How can s2 "know the future"? Bootstrapping, again

Qtarget(s2, ·) is the critic's current guess of everything after s2. At the start it is rubbish (our critic said 0.1 when the truth was −455). Each update copies a little real reward r into the guesses, and the knowledge spreads backwards through the 100,000 rows, just as the goal's value rippled back one square per game on FrozenLake. A network spreads it faster, because nudging one state also nudges similar states.

② The actor: climb the critic's hill (the chain rule)

The actor wants μ(s) to be the torque with the biggest Q. So it changes its weights θ to make Q(s, μ(s)) bigger. How does Q change when one weight θ changes? θ changes the torque a, and the torque changes Q. A chain of two links, so school's chain rule:

 ∂Q        ∂Q        ∂a
────  =  ────  ×  ────            θ ← θ + learning rate × ∂Q/∂θ      (gradient ASCENT)
 ∂θ        ∂a        ∂θ
 ▲          ▲         ▲
 how Q      slope of  how the torque moves when
 reacts     the       you change this weight
 to θ       critic's  (comes from the actor network)
            hill

In code we write actor_loss = −Q(s, μ(s)).mean() and call backward(); PyTorch applies the chain rule through every layer for us. Minimising −Q is the same as maximising Q.

Worked example 2: a one-weight actor (by hand)

Toy actor a = θ · s. Toy critic (one hill) Q(s, a) = 5 − (a − 2s)², best torque = 2s. State s = 1, start θ = 0.5, learning rate 0.1.

a       = θ · s = 0.5 × 1 = 0.5
∂Q/∂a   = −2(a − 2s) = −2(0.5 − 2) = +3.0        hill rises to the right
∂a/∂θ   = s = 1                                   one unit more θ → one unit more torque
∂Q/∂θ   = 3.0 × 1 = 3.0                           (PyTorch autograd also gives 3.0000)
θ_new   = 0.5 + 0.1 × 3.0 = 0.8

step   θ        a        Q        ∂Q/∂θ
 0     0.5000   0.5000   2.7500   3.0000
 1     0.8000   0.8000   3.5600   2.4000
 2     1.0400   1.0400   4.0784   1.9200
 3     1.2320   1.2320   4.4102   1.5360        → creeps toward θ = 2 (top), slower as the hill flattens

Worked example 3: the real actor's tanh layer

Our actor ends with a = 2 · tanh(z) so the torque can never leave [−2, 2]. Toy version: z = θ·s, same critic, θ = 0.5, s = 1. One more link in the chain:

tanh(0.5) = 0.4621        a = 2 × 0.4621 = 0.9242        Q = 5 − (0.9242 − 2)² = 3.8427
∂Q/∂a = −2(a − 2)               = −2(0.9242 − 2)          = 2.1515
∂a/∂θ = 2·(1 − tanh²(z))·s      = 2 × 0.7864 × 1          = 1.5729   (derivative of tanh is 1 − tanh²)
∂Q/∂θ = 2.1515 × 1.5729         = 3.3841                          (autograd: 3.3841)
Notice

If z is large, tanh(z) ≈ ±1 and 1 − tanh² ≈ 0: the actor's gradient almost vanishes. A torque pinned at exactly ±2 learns slowly. You'll see our actors spend a lot of time at ±2 (bang-bang swinging), which is fine for Pendulum.

③ The target networks: soft (Polyak) updates

θtarget  ←  (1 − τ) · θtarget  +  τ · θ        τ = 0.005: keep 99.5% of the old, take 0.5% of the new

Worked example 4: one weight, followed over time

online weight = 0.80 (pretend it stays fixed), target weight starts at 0.50
after 1 step:     0.995 × 0.50 + 0.005 × 0.80 = 0.5015
after 10 steps:   0.5147
after 100 steps:  0.6183
after 138 steps:  0.6498      ← the gap (0.30) has halved: ln 0.5 / ln 0.995 ≈ 138 steps
after 1000 steps: 0.7980      ← almost caught up
by hand with τ = 0.1:  0.50 → 0.5300 → 0.5570 → 0.5813

Formula: after n steps the gap is 0.30 × 0.995n, a geometric sequence. The target follows the online network smoothly, about 140 steps behind, so the Bellman target doesn't jump around.

④ Exploration: wobble the torque

The actor is deterministic: same state, same torque. Without randomness it would never try anything new (like Q-learning with ε = 0). DDPG adds Gaussian noise while training:

a = clip( μ(s) + noise,  −2, +2 )       noise ~ Normal(0, σ = 0.1 × 2 = 0.2)
e.g. μ(s) = 1.30, noise drawn = −0.17  →  a = 1.13 is sent to env.step and stored in the buffer

Plus the first 1,000 steps use completely random torques to fill the buffer. At test time: no noise (like evaluate() using pure argmax in FrozenLake).

06TD3: three fixes, with numbers

Twin Delayed DDPG (Fujimoto et al., 2018). Same code as DDPG plus three if self.td3: blocks.

The problem: the actor hunts for the critic's mistakes

The critic is a learned guess, so some torques are scored too high by luck. The actor is trained to find the torque with the highest score, so it is drawn exactly to those lucky errors. Then the Bellman target uses that inflated score, the error is copied into the next state's value, and so on. Errors that go up get chosen; errors that go down get ignored. On average: too optimistic.

Worked example 5: max of noisy guesses

True value of 5 torques:           10.0   10.0   10.0   10.0   10.0
Critic 1 (with random errors):     10.8    9.5   10.2    9.1   11.3  → max = 11.3   (+1.3 too high!)
Critic 2 (its own random errors):   9.6   10.4   10.9   10.3    9.8
TD3 target uses min(11.3, 9.8) = 9.8                               (−0.2: slightly low, much closer)

hand_examples.py repeats this 100,000 times with 10 actions and errors of size 1:

Estimate used in the targetAverage error (true value 0)
One critic, take the max (DDPG / DQN style)+1.540 (always too high)
Pick with critic 1, score with min(critic 1, critic 2) (TD3)−0.049

And bootstrapping compounds it: each target contains the next state's inflated value. With γ = 0.99 a bias of +1.54 per step can grow toward 1.54 / (1 − 0.99) = 154. A slightly pessimistic critic is much safer than an optimistic one.

Fix 1 · Twin critics, take the min ("clipped double Q")

y = r + γ · min( Q1target(s2, a2), Q2target(s2, a2) )         both critics learn toward the SAME y

Two critics start from different random weights, so they make different mistakes. It's rare that both are too high at the same torque.

Fix 2 · Delayed actor updates

Update the actor (and the target networks) only every 2nd critic update. Climbing a hill that is still being drawn is wasted effort; let the judge settle a bit first. In code: if self.td3 and self.updates % 2 != 0: return.

Fix 3 · Target policy smoothing

A critic can grow a thin, sharp, wrong spike at one exact torque. If the target always asks about exactly μtarget(s2), it keeps hitting the spike. So TD3 jiggles the target torque a little, which averages Q over a small neighbourhood:

a2 = clip( μtarget(s2) + clip(ε, −1.0, +1.0),  −2, +2 )       ε ~ Normal(0, 0.2 × 2 = 0.4)

Worked example 6:  μtarget(s2) = 1.6
   noise drawn +0.3   → inside ±1.0, keep +0.3  → a2 = 1.6 + 0.3 = 1.9
   noise drawn −1.4   → clip to −1.0            → a2 = 1.6 − 1.0 = 0.6
Don't mix up the two noises

Exploration noise (σ = 0.2) goes on the torque sent to the real game. Smoothing noise (σ = 0.4, clipped) goes on the torque a2 inside the Bellman target; the game never sees it.

07SAC: the curious, random actor

Soft Actor-Critic (Haarnoja et al., 2018). The actor outputs a bell curve; the reward includes a bonus for keeping it wide.

① A bell curve instead of one torque

SAC's actor outputs two numbers per action: a mean (centre) and log σ (log of the spread; the log keeps σ positive). The torque is sampled, then squashed:

② The reparameterisation trick, simply

"Draw a random torque from a bell curve" has no slope: you can't ask "how does a dice roll change if I move the mean?" But "take a standard dice roll ε, multiply by σ, add the mean" gives exactly the same kind of random numbers, and it is just algebra: u = mean + σ·ε. With ε fixed after drawing, ∂u/∂mean = 1 and ∂u/∂σ = ε. Now the chain rule from chapter 05 works again.

Worked example 7: one sample, by hand

actor says  mean = 0.3,  σ = 0.5          dice gives ε = 0.8
u        = 0.3 + 0.5 × 0.8                = 0.700
tanh(u)  = tanh(0.7)                      = 0.6044
torque   = 2 × 0.6044                     = 1.2087
slopes   ∂torque/∂mean = 2(1 − tanh²u)    = 1.2695
         ∂torque/∂σ    = 2(1 − tanh²u)·ε  = 1.0156          (PyTorch agrees)

③ The curiosity bonus: −α · log π

π(a|s) is how likely the actor was to pick that torque (a probability density). log π is very negative for an unlikely, surprising pick and large for a pick it was sure about. SAC adds −α · log π to the value: random policies earn more. On average −log π is the entropy H, "how spread out":

Spread σ of a bell curve2.01.00.50.10.01
Entropy H = ½ ln(2πe σ²)+2.112+1.419+0.726−0.884−3.186

Wider = more entropy = bigger bonus. "Reward for staying curious": the policy only narrows its bell curve where being precise clearly pays more than the bonus. Benefits: it explores without hand-made noise, it doesn't bet everything on one torque the critic might be wrong about, and it keeps several good options alive.

Worked example 8: log π for the sample above (with the tanh correction)

log of the bell curve at u:   −½ε² − ln σ − ½ln(2π) = −0.320 + 0.6931 − 0.9189 = −0.5458
tanh squash correction:       − ln(1 − tanh²u) = − ln(0.6347)              = +0.4545
log π(a|s)                    = −0.5458 + 0.4545                            = −0.0913

Why a correction? tanh squeezes the line into (−1, 1), piling probability up near the ends, so the density changes. 1 − tanh² is how much tanh squeezes at that point (the same derivative as in example 3).

④ SAC's Bellman target ("soft" target)

y = r + γ·(1 − done)·[ min( Q1targ(s2, a2), Q2targ(s2, a2) )  −  α · log π(a2|s2) ]
                       ▲ twin critics, like TD3                    ▲ curiosity bonus for s2
    a2 = a FRESH sample from the CURRENT actor in s2 (not from the buffer, no target actor)

Worked example 9: a soft target

r = −1.0,  Q1′ = −15.0,  Q2′ = −15.6,  α = 0.2,  log π(a2|s2) = −0.09
min = −15.6,   bonus −α·log π = −0.2 × (−0.09) = +0.018
y = −1.0 + 0.99 × (−15.6 + 0.018) = −16.4262
   if log π had been +1.5 (very sure):      bonus −0.30  → y = −16.7410
   if log π had been −2.0 (very surprised): bonus +0.40  → y = −16.0480

⑤ The actor's goal and the temperature α

actor:   maximise   min(Q1, Q2)(s, ã) − α · log π(ã|s)      ã = fresh reparameterised sample
α:       if the policy's entropy falls below the target (−1 for one action), raise α; else lower it

α is a thermostat for curiosity. In our run it started at 1.0 and the code lowered it to 0.047 by the end, as the agent became sure what to do (chapter 10 shows the chart). At test time SAC uses the bell's centre, 2·tanh(mean): no randomness.

08The algorithms: flow + pseudocode

One loop for all three; only the "learn" box differs.

DDPG (TD3 changes in blue) 
init actor μ, critic Q1 (+Q2), copies
for each step:
  a = clip(μ(s) + N(0, 0.2))
  s2, r = env.step(a); store row
  batch = 128 random rows
  a2 = μ′(s2) + clip(N(0,0.4), ±1)
  y = r + γ·Q1′(s2,a2)
      → γ·min(Q1′, Q2′)(s2,a2)
  Q1 (Q2) ← toward y
  every 2nd update only:
    μ ← uphill on Q1(s, μ(s))
    all targets ← soft update τ
SAC 
init actor π, critics Q1, Q2, copies, α
for each step:
  a ~ π(·|s)          (no extra noise)
  s2, r = env.step(a); store row
  batch = 128 random rows
  a2 ~ π(·|s2), with log π(a2|s2)
  y = r + γ·[min(Q1′,Q2′)(s2,a2)
             − α·log π(a2|s2)]
  Q1, Q2 ← toward y
  π ← uphill on min Q − α·log π
  α ← up if too sure, down if not
  critic targets ← soft update τ

② DDPG / TD3: the critic (exactly DQN's target, with the actor in place of max)

with torch.no_grad():
    a2 = self.mu(self.actor_t, s2)                         # target actor's torque in s2
    if self.td3:                                           # TD3 fix 3: smoothing
        eps = (torch.randn_like(a2) * self.target_noise * self.max_action)
        eps = eps.clamp(-self.noise_clip * self.max_action, self.noise_clip * self.max_action)
        a2 = (a2 + eps).clamp(-self.max_action, self.max_action)
    q2 = self.critic1_t(torch.cat([s2, a2], 1))
    if self.td3:                                           # TD3 fix 1: min of twins
        q2 = torch.min(q2, self.critic2_t(torch.cat([s2, a2], 1)))
    target = r + self.gamma * (1 - done) * q2              # Bellman: r + γ·Q(s2, a2)
critic_loss = F.mse_loss(self.critic1(torch.cat([s, a], 1)), target)

④ SAC: the reparameterised, squashed sample

mean, log_std = self.actor(s).chunk(2, dim=-1)          # the actor outputs TWO numbers
std = log_std.clamp(-5, 2).exp()
eps = torch.randn_like(mean)                           # the dice roll, outside the network
u = mean + std * eps                                   # differentiable in mean and std
a = torch.tanh(u)                                      # squash into (−1, 1), then × 2
log_prob = (-0.5 * eps**2 - log_std - 0.5 * math.log(2 * math.pi)).sum(-1, keepdim=True)
log_prob = log_prob - torch.log(1 - a**2 + 1e-6).sum(-1, keepdim=True)   # squash correction

⑤ SAC: soft target, curious actor, self-tuning α

target = r + self.gamma * (1 - done) * (q2 - alpha * logp2)     # + curiosity bonus

LineMeaning
buf.add(…, float(terminated))Pendulum never really "ends"; its 200-step limit is a time-out, so the future always counts. Same lesson as A2C and PPO.
self.mu(self.actor_t, s2)Where DQN wrote max Q(s2), DDPG asks the target actor for "the best torque" in s2.
-self.critic1(…μ(s)…)Maximising Q by minimising −Q. backward() applies ∂Q/∂a · ∂a/∂θ. Only the actor's optimiser steps, so the critic is not changed by this loss.
soft_update(…, tau)Polyak: target ← (1 − τ)·target + τ·online, every update, instead of DQN's copy every 500 steps.
u = mean + std * epsThe reparameterisation trick: randomness comes from ε, so gradients flow into mean and std.
q2 - alpha * logp2The soft Bellman target: future value + a bonus for being random (−log π is large when π is spread out).
target_entropy = −1"Keep about this much randomness." α rises when the actor becomes too certain, and falls when it's random enough.

13Real-world problems

When these methods fit

✔ The action is one or more real numbers (torques, angles, speeds, amounts). ✔ Data is expensive: these are off-policy methods with a replay buffer, so every experience is reused many times. ✔ A simulator exists for safe practice. ✘ Very fast simulators with many parallel copies often favour PPO instead; discrete actions favour DQN.

1 · A robot learning to walk (well documented)

Statejoint angles and speeds, body tilt, foot contact sensors (~20–40 numbers)
Actiona target torque for each of 8–12 motors: 8–12 real numbers at once
Reward+forward speed − energy used − large penalty for falling
Why SACDiscretising 12 motors × 41 levels = 4112 actions (impossible for DQN). SAC is sample-efficient (replay) and its entropy bonus keeps exploring gaits. The SAC authors reported a real four-legged robot learning to walk in about two hours of real-world experience.
PitfallsFalling damages hardware: use safety limits and a human with a stop button. The sim-to-real gap: a policy perfect in simulation may fail on real motors, so add noise to the simulator ("domain randomisation").

2 · A drone holding its position in the wind

Stateposition error, velocity, tilt angles, angular speeds, battery
Action4 motor thrusts (continuous)
Reward−(distance from target point)² − 0.01·(thrust changes)² (smoothness)
Why TD3 / SACContinuous, coupled actions; train in a simulator with random wind, then test carefully. Research groups have trained drone controllers this way ("could be used for").

3 · Office temperature control with a continuous valve

Stateroom and outside temperature, time of day, occupancy, energy price
Actionvalve opening 0–100% and fan speed: two real numbers
Reward−energy cost − 10 × (degrees outside the comfort band)
WhyThe SARSA booklet discretised this into 4 settings; a continuous controller can make fine adjustments. Off-policy learning can reuse months of logged building data.
PitfallLearned controllers can do odd things in rare states (like our DDPG's ±2 rattle near the top). Keep a simple safe fallback rule.

15Clearing up the confusions

What does the DDPG actor output: a probability or an action?

An action: one torque number (deterministic). Exploration noise is added outside the network. SAC's actor outputs a bell curve (mean and σ) and samples from it.

Is the critic's Q the same as DQN's Q?

Same meaning, but with the action as an input instead of one output per action: Q(s, a) takes 3 state numbers + 1 torque and returns one number.

Where did "max over actions" go?

Into the actor. μ(s) is trained to output the torque that maximises Q(s, ·), so the target uses Q(s2, μtarget(s2)) instead of max.

Why does the actor need the critic's gradient ∂Q/∂a?

The world gives no gradient (we can't differentiate a pendulum). The critic is a smooth, differentiable stand-in: its slope says "a slightly bigger torque would be better", and the chain rule passes that into the actor's weights.

Which state is updated?

As always: the states in the sampled batch (the states you left). s2 is only read to build the target. The actor is updated at the states s, never at s2.

Why two critics and the smaller one?

Each critic makes noisy errors; the actor actively seeks torques where the critic is too optimistic. Taking the min of two independent critics cancels most of that optimism (simulation: +1.54 error with one critic vs −0.05 with the min).

Is SAC's "entropy" the same as in A2C/PPO?

Same idea, "how spread out is the policy". But SAC puts it inside the reward (the soft Bellman target), and tunes its weight α automatically.

Why tanh?

The motor only accepts torques in [−2, 2]. tanh squeezes any number into (−1, 1), × 2 gives a legal torque, and it's smooth, so gradients still flow.

Why does the DDPG pendulum "rattle" at the top?

Its policy map has a sharp border near upright, so tiny angle changes flip the torque between +2 and −2. It still balances (the average torque is about 0), but it's not smooth. SAC's smoother policy holds still. Nobody rewarded smoothness, so the agent didn't care.

Is the critic's estimate of the start state correct?

By the end, roughly: about −75 to −98 vs a real −87. Early on it was badly wrong (0 vs −455), because a critic learns from bootstrapped targets, which start out wrong.

Why are these called off-policy if the actor keeps changing?

The critic's target only needs (s, a, r, s2) from the world, plus the current actor's choice at s2. Old moves from older actors are still valid evidence about the world, which is why replay works.

Four sentences to remember
  1. With continuous actions you can't take a max over all actions, so an actor learns to output the best action and is trained by climbing the critic's slope (chain rule: ∂Q/∂a · ∂a/∂θ).
  2. DDPG = DQN's critic (replay, target networks, Bellman target) + a deterministic actor + exploration noise.
  3. TD3 adds twin critics (take the min), a delayed actor and target smoothing, all against over-estimation.
  4. SAC uses a random (squashed Gaussian) actor and adds an entropy bonus inside the reward, with α tuned automatically.
