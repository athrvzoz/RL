01The big picture

If you remember only one page, remember this one.

In FrozenLake and Taxi the agent had a short list of actions: left, down, right, up. To act well it looked at one row of the Q-table and took the biggest number (argmax). To learn, it used max Q[s2]. Both need you to try every action and compare.

Now imagine the action is "how hard to push the motor": any number between −2 and +2. There is no row with 4 boxes. There are infinitely many torques: 0.5, 0.51, 0.5013… You can't try every number. This booklet is about the three methods that solve that problem.

What problem do they solve that your previous methods couldn't?

Where you used itQ-learning / DQN lineWhat breaks for a torque in [−2, 2]
Choosing an actionargmax(Q[s]): compare all actionsInfinitely many actions to compare
Bellman targetr + γ · max Q[s2]"max over all torques" is itself a search problem
Storing Qone box per (state, action)states and actions are both real numbers: no boxes
The one sentence

Instead of searching for the best action every time, train a second network, the actor, that outputs the best action directly, and improve it by asking the critic Q(s, a) "which way is uphill?" (that's the chain rule ∂Q/∂a · ∂a/∂θ).

And the three names in one line each

  • DDPG: deterministic actor μ(s) + critic Q(s, a). Explore by adding a little noise to the torque.
  • TD3: DDPG + twin critics (take the smaller), delayed actor updates, and smoothed targets. "Twin Delayed DDPG".
  • SAC: the actor outputs a bell curve of torques, not one torque, and gets a bonus reward for staying random ("staying curious"). Twin critics like TD3.
Our test bench: Pendulum-v1

State = [cos θ, sin θ, θ̇] (3 numbers). Action = 1 real number: torque.
Reward every step = −(θ² + 0.1·θ̇² + 0.001·a²): 0 when perfectly upright and still, about −16 at worst.
Game = 200 steps, then truncated (never "terminated").
The motor is too weak to lift the pole directly, so the agent must learn to swing it up, then balance.

02Recap: nets, replay, targets, actor & critic

The four ideas from DQN and actor-critic that this booklet builds on. Other booklets cover them in depth; here's what you need.

① A neural network is a Q-table that can fill in gaps

Your FrozenLake Q-table had 16 × 4 = 64 boxes. Pendulum's state is three real numbers, so there are infinitely many states. You can't have a box for each. A neural network is a function with adjustable numbers inside it (weights, called θ). You feed in numbers, it multiplies and adds, and out comes a number:

Learning = changing the weights so the output gets closer to a target. Instead of Q[s,a] += α·(target − Q[s,a]), we compute the loss (target − Q)², and gradient descent moves every weight a little in the direction that shrinks it. Same idea, "nudge toward the target", but it changes many weights at once, so it also changes the answers for similar states. That's the "fill in gaps" part.

② Replay buffer: a notebook of past moves

Every step we write one row (s, a, r, s2, done) into a big memory (100,000 rows). To learn, we pick 128 random rows and nudge on all of them. Why random? Consecutive steps are almost the same state (the pendulum moves a tiny bit each step). Learning from 128 nearly identical rows would push the net in one direction too hard. Random rows from many games are varied, like a teacher mixing questions from all chapters. Also, every row gets reused many times, so we need far fewer real steps.

③ Target network: don't chase your own tail

The Bellman target r + γ·Q(s2, …) uses the critic itself. If the same network both makes the target and moves toward it, every nudge also moves the target: like a dog chasing its tail. Fix: keep a slow copy of the network, the target network (written Q′ or Qtarget), and use only that copy to compute targets. DQN copies the weights over every few thousand steps. DDPG/TD3/SAC use a soft update instead: every step the copy moves just 0.5% of the way toward the real network (chapter 05 has the numbers).

④ Actor and critic: two jobs, two networks

Critic · "the judge"

Q(s, a): "if I'm in state s and push with torque a, then play well, how much total reward do I expect?" One number. Exactly the Q from Q-learning, but as a network that takes the torque as an input.

Actor · "the player"

μ(s): "in state s, push with this torque." It outputs an action. It replaces the argmax you used to choose moves. The critic tells it which way to change.

In A2C/PPO (other booklets) the actor outputs probabilities and learns from whole games it just played (on-policy). Here the actor learns from the critic's slope on rows from the replay buffer, including very old ones (off-policy). That's why these methods need far fewer steps.

Calculated vs given: the same rule as before

Given by the world: the reward r, the next state s2, whether the game ended. Calculated by us: the target, the critic's Q, the actor's torque. Learned (slowly changed): the weights θ of the actor and critic. Nobody gives the agent the physics of the pendulum: it is model-free, just like Q-learning.

04Intuition: you can't try every number

Why the argmax breaks, and the idea that replaces it.

Why not just chop the torque into pieces?

You could pretend the torque is a list: −2.0, −1.9, …, +2.0. That's 41 choices, and DQN could handle that for one motor. But real machines have many motors, and every motor's choices multiply (your code, hand_examples.py, part 1):

Motors (joints)Choices eachActions to compare in every argmax
1 (pendulum)4141
2411,681
34168,921
7 (a robot arm)41194,754,273,881

And steps of 0.1 are still rough: a steering angle of 0.73° vs 0.74° can matter. So we need a method where the action is calculated, not searched for.

Everyday analogy: the shower knob

You step into a hotel shower with a smooth knob. You don't try all 1,000 positions. You:

  1. turn it somewhere (actor: "my guess is here"),
  2. feel the water: "a bit too cold" (critic: a judgement, and crucially a direction),
  3. turn it a little toward hot (gradient ascent),
  4. repeat. Sometimes wiggle it to check (exploration noise).

The secret ingredient is in step 2: the feedback doesn't just say "good" or "bad", it says which way to turn. In maths, "which way is uphill, and how steep?" is a derivative. The critic is a smooth function of the torque, so we can take its derivative ∂Q/∂a. That's the whole trick.

Catch

The critic's "feel" is learned, so at first it is wrong. Actor and critic improve together, and most of TD3's and SAC's fixes are about not trusting a wrong critic too much.

The picture: list vs hill

Tiny hand example: one climb step

Suppose the critic (for one state) says Q(a) = 5 − (a − 2)², a hill with its top at a = 2. The actor currently says a = 0.5.

Q at a = 0.5        5 − (0.5 − 2)²  = 5 − 2.25 = 2.75
slope at a = 0.5    dQ/da = −2(a − 2) = −2(−1.5) = +3       positive: uphill is to the RIGHT
step (size 0.1)     a ← 0.5 + 0.1 × 3 = 0.8
Q at a = 0.8        5 − (0.8 − 2)²  = 5 − 1.44 = 3.56        higher than 2.75: it worked

Nobody tried a = 0.6, 0.7, … The slope alone said "go right, and quite a lot". That is the core of DDPG. In the real algorithm the actor is a network, so we nudge its weights, not the torque directly; chapter 05 adds that one extra link with the chain rule.

Connect it to what you know

In Q-learning, argmax(Q[s]) was a tiny "search". In DDPG the actor is that search, learned once and then answered instantly: μ(s) ≈ argmaxa Q(s, a). Everything else (Bellman target, replay, target network) stays as in DQN.
