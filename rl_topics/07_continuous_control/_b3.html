LineMeaning
buf.add(…, float(terminated))Pendulum never really "ends"; its 200-step limit is a time-out, so the future always counts. Same lesson as A2C and PPO.
self.mu(self.actor_t, s2)Where DQN wrote max Q(s2), DDPG asks the target actor for "the best torque" in s2.
-self.critic1(…μ(s)…)Maximising Q by minimising −Q. backward() applies ∂Q/∂a · ∂a/∂θ. Only the actor's optimiser steps, so the critic is not changed by this loss.
soft_update(…, tau)Polyak: target ← (1 − τ)·target + τ·online, every update, instead of DQN's copy every 500 steps.
u = mean + std * epsThe reparameterisation trick: randomness comes from ε, so gradients flow into mean and std.
q2 - alpha * logp2The soft Bellman target: future value + a bonus for being random (−log π is large when π is spread out).
target_entropy = −1"Keep about this much randomness." α rises when the actor becomes too certain, and falls when it's random enough.

13Real-world problems

When these methods fit

✔ The action is one or more real numbers (torques, angles, speeds, amounts). ✔ Data is expensive: these are off-policy methods with a replay buffer, so every experience is reused many times. ✔ A simulator exists for safe practice. ✘ Very fast simulators with many parallel copies often favour PPO instead; discrete actions favour DQN.

1 · A robot learning to walk (well documented)

Statejoint angles and speeds, body tilt, foot contact sensors (~20–40 numbers)
Actiona target torque for each of 8–12 motors: 8–12 real numbers at once
Reward+forward speed − energy used − large penalty for falling
Why SACDiscretising 12 motors × 41 levels = 4112 actions (impossible for DQN). SAC is sample-efficient (replay) and its entropy bonus keeps exploring gaits. The SAC authors reported a real four-legged robot learning to walk in about two hours of real-world experience.
PitfallsFalling damages hardware: use safety limits and a human with a stop button. The sim-to-real gap: a policy perfect in simulation may fail on real motors, so add noise to the simulator ("domain randomisation").

2 · A drone holding its position in the wind

Stateposition error, velocity, tilt angles, angular speeds, battery
Action4 motor thrusts (continuous)
Reward−(distance from target point)² − 0.01·(thrust changes)² (smoothness)
Why TD3 / SACContinuous, coupled actions; train in a simulator with random wind, then test carefully. Research groups have trained drone controllers this way ("could be used for").

3 · Office temperature control with a continuous valve

Stateroom and outside temperature, time of day, occupancy, energy price
Actionvalve opening 0–100% and fan speed: two real numbers
Reward−energy cost − 10 × (degrees outside the comfort band)
WhyThe SARSA booklet discretised this into 4 settings; a continuous controller can make fine adjustments. Off-policy learning can reuse months of logged building data.
PitfallLearned controllers can do odd things in rare states (like our DDPG's ±2 rattle near the top). Keep a simple safe fallback rule.

15Clearing up the confusions

What does the DDPG actor output: a probability or an action?

An action: one torque number (deterministic). Exploration noise is added outside the network. SAC's actor outputs a bell curve (mean and σ) and samples from it.

Is the critic's Q the same as DQN's Q?

Same meaning, but with the action as an input instead of one output per action: Q(s, a) takes 3 state numbers + 1 torque and returns one number.

Where did "max over actions" go?

Into the actor. μ(s) is trained to output the torque that maximises Q(s, ·), so the target uses Q(s2, μtarget(s2)) instead of max.

Why does the actor need the critic's gradient ∂Q/∂a?

The world gives no gradient (we can't differentiate a pendulum). The critic is a smooth, differentiable stand-in: its slope says "a slightly bigger torque would be better", and the chain rule passes that into the actor's weights.

Which state is updated?

As always: the states in the sampled batch (the states you left). s2 is only read to build the target. The actor is updated at the states s, never at s2.

Why two critics and the smaller one?

Each critic makes noisy errors; the actor actively seeks torques where the critic is too optimistic. Taking the min of two independent critics cancels most of that optimism (simulation: +1.54 error with one critic vs −0.05 with the min).

Is SAC's "entropy" the same as in A2C/PPO?

Same idea, "how spread out is the policy". But SAC puts it inside the reward (the soft Bellman target), and tunes its weight α automatically.

Why tanh?

The motor only accepts torques in [−2, 2]. tanh squeezes any number into (−1, 1), × 2 gives a legal torque, and it's smooth, so gradients still flow.

Why does the DDPG pendulum "rattle" at the top?

Its policy map has a sharp border near upright, so tiny angle changes flip the torque between +2 and −2. It still balances (the average torque is about 0), but it's not smooth. SAC's smoother policy holds still. Nobody rewarded smoothness, so the agent didn't care.

Is the critic's estimate of the start state correct?

By the end, roughly: about −75 to −98 vs a real −87. Early on it was badly wrong (0 vs −455), because a critic learns from bootstrapped targets, which start out wrong.

Why are these called off-policy if the actor keeps changing?

The critic's target only needs (s, a, r, s2) from the world, plus the current actor's choice at s2. Old moves from older actors are still valid evidence about the world, which is why replay works.

critic targets ← Polyak(τ)
Four sentences to remember
  1. With continuous actions you can't take a max over all actions, so an actor learns to output the best action and is trained by climbing the critic's slope (chain rule: ∂Q/∂a · ∂a/∂θ).
  2. DDPG = DQN's critic (replay, target networks, Bellman target) + a deterministic actor + exploration noise.
  3. TD3 adds twin critics (take the min), a delayed actor and target smoothing, all against over-estimation.
  4. SAC uses a random (squashed Gaussian) actor and adds an entropy bonus inside the reward, with α tuned automatically.

  11. PPO is on-policy: each batch of experience is used for a few epochs, then thrown away. SAC keeps every trial in its replay buffer and learns from it again and again, so it needs far fewer expensive trials.
  12. Pendulum's physics plus the 1,000 random warm-up moves and the changing start positions already give the replay buffer a lot of variety. In harder tasks, no noise usually means the agent gets stuck.
