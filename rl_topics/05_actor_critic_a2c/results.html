Inside the critic: what it learned

  • The early critic gives every state about 25: it hasn't learned to tell states apart yet.
  • The final critic knows a calm pole is worth the maximum, 1/(1 − 0.99) = 100, and a fast-spinning one much less: "a fast-falling pole is bad news". That's exactly the information the actor needs to judge its moves.
  • Along a perfect 500-step game, the final critic says V ≈ 100 at every step, even at step 499. That's correct: the time limit is not a real end (chapter 09's final_obs trick).

B · n-step: how far to look before trusting the critic

n_stepsupdatesexam score, seed 0 / seed 1
1 (pure TD)10,000500.0 / 500.0
5 (default)2,000251.6 / 385.0
20500146.6 / 358.9

With the same 80,000 steps, n = 1 makes 5× more updates than n = 5, and on CartPole that won clearly. The lesson isn't "n = 1 is always best". With the same number of steps, a smaller n means more, smaller learning steps, and CartPole's critic becomes accurate quickly, so trusting it early pays off.

C · Separate networks vs a shared body

setupexam score, seed 0 / seed 1
separate actor and critic251.6 / 385.0
shared body, two heads122.2 / 115.2

Sharing layers saves computation, but the critic's large squared errors (values up to 100) can drown out the actor's small signal in the shared weights. Here separate networks won clearly.

This is the whole booklet in one experiment: replacing the noisy return G with the critic's advantage made the signal about 7× less noisy and allowed 3× more updates (every 5 steps instead of once per game). A2C reached a perfect exam on every seed; REINFORCE didn't on any. (One honest detail: A2C's score curve moved around more between checkpoints, 38.8 vs 27.2, because it climbs to high scores and its swings up there are larger. "Less noisy signal" doesn't mean "perfectly smooth curve".)
