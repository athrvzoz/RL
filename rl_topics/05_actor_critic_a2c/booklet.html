01The big picture

Value-based and policy-based, finally working together.

You now know both families of RL:

  • Value-based (Q-learning, DQN): learn how good things are, using the Bellman equation. Low noise, learns every step, but can't hold a random or continuous policy.
  • Policy-based (REINFORCE): learn the action probabilities directly. Flexible, but very noisy, and must wait for each game to end.

Actor-critic uses one of each, as two cooperating networks:

The one sentence

The actor picks actions with probabilities; the critic learns V(s) with a Bellman target; after every few steps the critic's surprise, δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s), tells the actor "that move was better (δ > 0) or worse (δ < 0) than usual", and the actor raises or lowers its probability accordingly.

A2C ("Advantage Actor-Critic") is the standard, simple version: several copies of the game run side by side, and both networks learn from all of them every few steps. Its relative A3C ran the copies on different computer threads that updated at different times (asynchronously); A2C waits for all of them, which is simpler and works just as well.

03Intuition: player and coach

REINFORCE: waits for the final result

A batsman plays a whole match. Only at the end does someone say "we lost, so every shot you played today was a bit bad". A great cover drive in a lost match gets blamed. A lucky edge in a won match gets praised. Very noisy feedback.

Actor-critic: a coach watching every ball

A coach knows what's "normal" from each situation. After each shot: "from there, I expected about 20 more runs; after that shot I now expect 26. That shot was +6 better than normal." Immediate, specific, much less noisy.

The coach (critic) also learns: it adjusts its expectations from what actually happens, with the same TD update you used in booklet 01. And it never chooses moves; it only judges them.

Why the coach must be fair: the advantage

In CartPole every step gives +1, so raw rewards say "everything is good". The advantage asks a better question: "better than what I expected from here?" A move that leads from a calm state (V = 100) into a wobbly one (V = 60) gets a negative advantage even though it earned +1.

04Recap: REINFORCE and its problem

Booklet 04 in one page.

policy πθ(a|s) = softmax(network(s))              probabilities, rolled like dice
REINFORCE:  θ ← θ + α · (Gt − b) · ∇log πθ(at|st)     after each finished game
            ▲ the real return-to-go minus a baseline
  • Good: unbiased (uses real returns), handles random and continuous policies.
  • Problem 1, noise: Gt adds up every reward until the end, including luck that had nothing to do with action at.
  • Problem 2, waiting: nothing is learned until the game ends. In a 500-step game, that's 500 steps of no learning.
  • Problem 3, a weak baseline: "the average return of this game" is the same number for every state, but some states are much better than others.
The fix, in one line

Replace (Gt − b) with an estimate of the advantage that uses a learned, state-specific baseline V(s):

Gt − b   →   r + γ·V(s′) − V(s)   =  δ   (TD error)

V(s) is a far better baseline than one average for the whole game, and using V(s′) means we can learn after one step instead of waiting for the end.

05The maths: advantage and TD error

Two losses, one network update.

The advantage

A(s, a)  =  Q(s, a)  −  V(s)
            ▲ value of doing a here    ▲ value of here, on average over the actor's choices

A > 0: a was better than the actor's average move here. A < 0: worse. A2C never learns Q; it estimates A from real rewards plus the critic:

1-step:  A ≈ δ = r + γ·V(s′) − V(s)                     (V(s′) = 0 if the game really ended)
n-step:  A ≈ r1 + γ·r2 + … + γn−1·rn + γn·V(sn) − V(s)

The critic's update (a TD / regression problem)

critic loss = ( target − V(s) )²          target = r + γ·V(s′)   (or the n-step version)
gradient step  ≈  V(s) ← V(s) + α · δ     exactly TD(0) from booklet 01

The actor's update (REINFORCE with the advantage)

actor loss = − log π(a|s) · A            (A is treated as a constant: .detach())
gradient step:  θ ← θ + α · A · ∇log π(a|s)
for a softmax actor:  ∇h log π(a) = one_hot(a) − π       (booklet 04, chapter 04)

Total loss (what the code minimises)

loss = actor_loss  +  0.5 × critic_loss  −  0.01 × entropy
       ▲ improve π    ▲ improve V            ▲ stay a bit unsure (chapter 07)
Which school maths?

Squared error and its derivative (the critic), log and the chain rule (the actor), and the exponential inside softmax. Nothing new beyond booklets 03 and 04: actor-critic is their combination.

GIVEN   π = [0.8, 0.2].   V(s) = 2.0,  V(s′) = 3.0,  r = 0.  took RIGHT (the 20% action)

Compare examples 2 and 3: the rare action moved more than 4× as much as the common one (0.0185 vs 0.0042) for a similar-sized δ. ∇log π = one_hot − π is large when π(a) is small: a surprise from a rarely chosen action is big news.

Example 4: the game ENDS (terminated)

07Entropy and n-step returns

Entropy: how unsure is the actor?

H(π) = − Σa π(a) · ln π(a)

π = [0.50, 0.50]   H = 0.693    (fully unsure: ln 2)
π = [0.60, 0.40]   H = 0.673
π = [0.80, 0.20]   H = 0.500
π = [0.90, 0.10]   H = 0.325
π = [0.99, 0.01]   H = 0.056    (almost certain)

Check one: H(0.8, 0.2) = −(0.8 × ln 0.8 + 0.2 × ln 0.2) = −(0.8 × (−0.223) + 0.2 × (−1.609)) = 0.178 + 0.322 = 0.500.

The loss subtracts 0.01 × H, so the actor gets a small reward for staying unsure. It stops the actor from becoming 100% certain too early and never trying the other move again: the job ε did in Q-learning.

n-step returns: how far to look before trusting the critic

Real output: 3 rewards of +1, then the critic's guess V(s3) = 5.0, γ = 0.9, built backwards:

R2 = r2 + 0.9 × V(s3)  = 1 + 0.9 × 5.00 = 5.500
R1 = r1 + 0.9 × R2     = 1 + 0.9 × 5.50 = 5.950
R0 = r0 + 0.9 × R1     = 1 + 0.9 × 5.95 = 6.355
nusesnoisebias (from a wrong critic)
11 real reward + guesslowesthighest
5 (our default)5 real rewards + guessmediummedium
∞all real rewards (REINFORCE)highestnone

The same dial as n-step TD in booklet 01. PPO's GAE (booklet 06) blends all n at once.

08The algorithm (A2C)

Pseudocode

actor πθ, critic Vφ;   start 8 copies of the game
repeat:
    for 5 steps, in every copy:  a ~ π(·|s);  s′, r = step(a);  store log π(a|s), V(s), r, ended
    R = V(slast)                                      # bootstrap from the critic
    for t = 5 … 1:   R = rt + γ·R·(1 − endedt);  returnt = R
    At = returnt − V(st)
    loss = mean(−log π(at|st)·At) + 0.5·mean(At²) − 0.01·mean(H)
    one gradient step on θ and φ

09The code, line by line

a2c.py: train_a2c()

① Play n steps in all copies

for _ in range(n_steps):
    logits, v = model(to_t(obs))                         # actor AND critic look at all 8 states
    dist = torch.distributions.Categorical(logits=logits)
    a = dist.sample()                                    # actor ROLLS a die
    obs, r, terminated, truncated, info = envs.step(a.numpy())
    if truncated.any():                                  # time limit: the future still counts
        for i in np.flatnonzero(truncated & ~terminated):
            r[i] += gamma * model.value(to_t(info["final_obs"][i])).item()
    logps.append(dist.log_prob(a)); values.append(v); ents.append(dist.entropy())

② n-step returns, backwards from the critic's guess

with torch.no_grad():
    R = model.value(to_t(obs))                           # bootstrap: V(state we stopped in)
for t in reversed(range(n_steps)):
    R = rewards[t] + gamma * R * (1 - dones[t])          # game over → no future
    returns.append(R)

③ Advantage and the three-part loss

advantage = returns - values
actor_loss = -(logps * advantage.detach()).mean()     # REINFORCE with A instead of G
critic_loss = advantage.pow(2).mean()                 # (target − V)², like DQN
loss = actor_loss + vf_coef * critic_loss - ent_coef * entropy
opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward()
nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 0.5)      # safety: no giant steps
opt.step()
LineMeaning
gym.make_vec(…, num_envs=8)8 CartPoles at once. Each update sees 40 steps from 8 different situations, so less correlated data (the same problem DQN's replay buffer solved).
info["final_obs"] trickAt the 500-step time limit the pole didn't fall, so its future isn't 0. We add γ·V(last real state) so the critic isn't fooled into thinking the game "ended".
advantage.detach()In the actor's loss, A is a fixed verdict. Without detach, the actor's loss would also push the critic around.
advantage.pow(2)The critic's squared error: return − V(s) is exactly "target − prediction".
- ent_coef * entropySubtracting entropy from the loss = rewarding uncertainty.
clip_grad_norm_(…, 0.5)Caps the size of each gradient step, a common safety belt in actor-critic.

12Real-world problems

When actor-critic fits

✔ You want a policy (random or continuous actions) but REINFORCE is too noisy. ✔ Long or never-ending tasks (learns every few steps). ✔ You can run many simulator copies in parallel. ✘ When data is expensive and you must reuse it, off-policy methods (DQN, SAC) are more sample-efficient.

1 · Game AI that must react continuously

Statepositions and speeds of the player, opponents and ball (a vector), or screen pixels
Actionmove / jump / shoot: discrete, chosen with probabilities
Reward+1 for scoring, −1 for conceding, small shaping rewards
Why A2CMany game copies run in parallel on one computer, exactly A2C's setting. A3C/A2C were benchmark methods on Atari games in the original papers. The critic gives feedback every few frames instead of only at the end of a level.

2 · Scheduling jobs on a data-centre's machines

Statequeue of waiting jobs (sizes, waiting times), free CPU and memory on each machine
Actionwhich job to start next on which machine (a probability for each choice)
Reward−(total waiting time) each step
Why actor-criticA never-ending task: jobs keep arriving, so Monte Carlo/REINFORCE can't wait for "the end". The critic learns how good a queue situation is; the actor learns which choices improve it. Research has explored RL schedulers like this ("could be used for").
PitfallMust be trained in a simulator that matches real job patterns.

3 · A robot learning to walk in simulation

Statejoint angles, speeds, body tilt, foot contacts
Actiona torque per joint: continuous, from a Gaussian actor (mean and spread per joint)
Reward+forward speed − energy used − big penalty for falling
WhyContinuous actions need a policy; walking runs for thousands of steps, so the critic's step-by-step advantage is essential. In practice PPO (booklet 06), an improved actor-critic, is the usual choice.

14Clearing up the confusions

Are the actor and critic two separate neural networks?

In our default, yes (shared=False). They can also share a body with two heads. Experiment C compares both.

Does the critic choose actions?

Never. It only outputs one number, V(s). The actor alone chooses, by rolling its dice.

Is the critic's V the same V as in value iteration?

Same meaning, "expected total reward from here", but for the actor's current policy, and learned from experience with TD, not computed from a rule book.

Why V(s) and not Q(s,a) for the critic?

The advantage needs "what did I expect here?" = V(s), and "what did I get?" = r + γV(s′). One number per state is easier to learn than one per action.

Is δ the same as Q-learning's surprise?

Same idea: target − old guess. Q-learning uses it to fix Q[s, a]. Actor-critic uses it twice: to fix V(s) and as the actor's advantage.

Which state is updated?

For the critic: V of the states it was in (the states you left). For the actor: the probabilities of the actions taken in those states. s′ is only read, for the target, as in every TD method.

If the critic is wrong, isn't the actor misled?

Yes, early on. That's the "bias" of bootstrapping. Both improve together; n-step returns reduce the damage by using more real rewards.

Why did the score drop around step 60,000 and then recover?

Policy methods can take a bad step: a large update makes the actor worse, and its new games teach the critic misleading things for a while. PPO's whole purpose is to prevent such big bad steps.

Why is greedy testing (500.0) much better than rolling dice (398.1)?

The actor still keeps some uncertainty (entropy ≈ 0.56), so sampled play makes occasional bad pushes. For evaluation we can just take its most likely action.

Why does the final critic say V ≈ 100 even at step 499?

Because the 500-step limit is a time-out, not a real end. The pole was still up, so from the critic's point of view the future would have continued. Max value = 1/(1 − 0.99) = 100.

A2C in 9 lines 
actor π, critic V; 8 game copies
repeat:
  play 5 steps in each copy (a ~ π)
  R = V(last state)
  for t backwards: R = r + γR(1−end)
  A = R − V(s)
  loss = −logπ·A + 0.5·A² − 0.01·H
  one gradient step (both nets)
Key formulas 
A(s,a) = Q(s,a) − V(s)
δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s)
critic: V += α δ    (loss A²)
actor:  θ += α A ∇log π(a|s)
∇_h log π(a) = one_hot(a) − π
H = −Σ π ln π
Four sentences to remember
  1. Actor-critic = a policy network (the player) + a value network (the coach).
  2. The critic learns V(s) with a TD/Bellman target; its surprise δ is the actor's advantage.
  3. The actor is REINFORCE with the advantage in place of the full return: less noise, and it learns every few steps.
  4. A2C runs several game copies together, adds an entropy bonus to keep exploring, and uses n-step returns to balance noise and bias.
